## 🇬🇧 English
### **Unconditional Friendliness: A Guide to Mettā Meditation**

There’s a specific mental training designed to clear the fog of aversion, hatred, irritability, or negative mental states in general and replace it with something the Buddhist tradition calls **Mettā**. While *Mitta* is a friend, *mettā* is the *state* of a friend. Often translated as benevolence (bene volere: wishing well) or loving-kindness, *Mettā* is described in the sources as a "beautiful mental state" that focuses specifically on the well-being of living beings.

It’s more than just being a "patient" person; it’s a proactive mode of non-aversion where you consciously choose to see others as likable and wish for their happiness. Here is a look at how you can develop this practice, from your first sitting to a global perspective.

#### **Getting Started: The "Easy" Win**
In the beginning, you don’t want to make things harder than they need to be. The practice usually starts by picking a **dear person** (*piya puggala*)—someone you already find it easy to wish happiness for. 

A few tips from the sources for your first subject:
*   **Keep it simple:** Choose someone currently alive and usually of the same sex to avoid complicated feelings like lust.
*   **The internal spark:** If you’re struggling to feel benevolence for others, start with yourself. Thinking *"May I be happy"* can help you find the right mindset before moving on to others.
*   **The Mantra:** Visualize the person or just hold them in your mind and establish the thought: *"May this person be happy"*.

#### **Breaking the Barriers**
The real "workout" of *Mettā* meditation happens when you start **breaking the barriers** (*sima*). This is the process of removing the mental walls we build between people we like and people we don’t. You move through four stages:
1.  **The Dear Person:** Your initial easy subject.
2.  **The Neutral Person:** Someone you know is alive but don't have strong feelings about (maybe a shopkeeper or a neighbor you've only seen in passing).
3.  **The Disliked Person:** Someone you find difficult to be around.
4.  **The Enemy:** Someone who has genuinely hurt you or those you love.

The goal is to reach a level of concentration where your wish for the "enemy’s" happiness is just as stable and sincere as your wish for your best friend's.

#### **Why Practice Mettā?**
Beyond just feeling better, *Mettā* has some heavy-duty benefits according to the sources:
*   **The "Mettā Effect":** Interestingly, your practice doesn't just change your own mind. It can actually help the person you are focusing on feel more comfortable and happy, depending on the power of your concentration.
*   **Concentration Power:** *Mettā* is a form of **Samatha** (serenity) meditation. It helps you overcome "hindrances" like hatred and restlessness, leading to deep states of absorption called *Jhanas*. Because *Mettā* thrives on pleasant feelings, it can take you through the first three *Jhanas*.
*   **High Merit:** In terms of spiritual "merit" (*punna*), *Mettā* is considered the highest form of merit one can acquire within the cycle of rebirth, as it serves as the foundation for other virtues like compassion and altruistic joy.

#### **The Path to Wisdom**
While *Mettā* is a powerful tool for peace, the sources remind us that it is a "vehicle". To reach the ultimate goal of liberation (*Nibbana*), one must eventually transition from *Samatha* to **Vipassana** (insight meditation). 

By using the calm, concentrated mind developed through *Mettā*, you can begin to see reality as it is—understanding that all things are impermanent and "non-self". 

**A final thought:** Try practicing *Mettā* for just a few minutes every day. Start with yourself, move to a friend, and eventually, try to see the whole world with that same "unconditional friendliness". As the talk concludes, may you be happy and find the highest benefit for yourself and others.
