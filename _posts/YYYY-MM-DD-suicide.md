# An Analytical Perspective on Suicide in the Buddhist Sāsana
> **Disclaimer:** This analysis is strictly for doctrinal and educational purposes to understand Theravada Buddhist psychology and Kamma. It does not encourage or condone suicide. If you or someone you know is struggling, please seek professional support.
> 
## 1. Defining the Core Difference: Killing vs. Suicide
In the *Sāsana*, standard killing (*pāṇātipāta*) is distinct from the act of ending one's own life.
 * **Killing Others:** This is always rooted in ***dosacitta*** (a mind state of aversion/hatred) and driven by ***vadhakacetanā*** (the volition to destroy/murder). Unintentional killing does not count as the kamma of killing.
 * **Suicide:** While typically driven by delusion and aversion, suicide is a broader psychological topic. It is **not necessarily** driven by *vadhakacetanā* or *dosacitta*. If it were always an act of aversion, several canonical and commentarial exceptions could not be explained.
## 2. Textual and Canonical Exceptions
The following cases demonstrate that ending one's own life can sometimes occur without *dosacitta*:
 * **Arahats Starving Themselves (*Sappadāsattheravatthu*):** Arahats do not destroy their lives with their own hands, but some have chosen to cease eating to allow natural death, entirely free of defilements.
 * **Self-Combustion:** In some extremely long-lifespan *sāsanas* (such as Koṇḍañña Buddha's), Arahats have been known to consciously combust their bodies using meditative *iddhi* (psychic powers).
 * **Attempts by Lower Ariyas (*Mahānāma the Sakya*):** *Ariyas* (noble disciples) are completely incapable of breaking the precept against killing. Yet, Mahānāma attempted suicide to escape a worse fate. If suicide were functionally identical to killing (*pāṇātipāta*), an *ariya* would be unable to attempt it.
 * **Bodhisatta’s Sacrifices (*Dānaparamatthapāramī*):** In Jātaka tales (such as the Wise Hare or the Tigress), the Bodhisatta gives up his life to feed others. This act of ultimate perfection (*pāramī*) is driven by ***kusalacitta* with *ñāṇasampayutta*** (a wholesome mind accompanied by wisdom), not aversion.
 * **Mahābodhisattas as Devas or Brahmas (*Adhimuttakālakiriya*):** High-level Bodhisattas residing in heavenly realms can consciously determine to end their lifespans early to be reborn as humans to fulfill *pāramī*. *Brahma* deities completely lack *dosacitta*, proving this choice is free of anger or aversion.
 * **Meditators with Jhāna (*Godhikasuttavaṇṇanā*):** A *puthujjana* (worldly person) who possesses *jhāna* is temporarily incapable of killing, yet history shows cases like Godhika who ended his life while balancing his mind.
 * **Vinaya Distinctions:** In the monastic code, the rule against killing a human being carries a ***pārājika*** (permanent expulsion) offense. Conversely, the rule regarding self-harm/suicide is treated separately and carries a ***dukkaṭa*** (an offense of wrong-doing). This structural separation in the *Vinaya* proves they are fundamentally different actions.
## 3. Monastic Consequences in the Present Sāsana
In our current era, the Buddha laid down a specific training rule prohibiting monks from taking their own lives.
If a monk commits suicide today, the mental volition of intentionally shattering their monastic vows occurs at the moment of the act. This creates ***āṇavītikkamantarāya*** (an obstruction resulting from transgressing the Buddha's command). Because this obstruction acts as an immediate barrier to both *sagga* (heavenly rebirth) and *magga* (noble attainments), a monk who commits suicide will **necessarily be reborn in a state of misery (*duggati*)**.
## 4. Karmic Realities for Laypeople (A Reply to Connor Donohoe)
While suicide can theoretically be done with a wholesome mind in rare cosmic circumstances, the reality for regular people is heavily weighted by unwholesome kamma.
 * **The Role of Aversion:** In the vast majority of everyday cases, people commit suicide out of depression, severe emotional pain, and wrong views. This terminal state of mind is heavy *dosacitta*, which almost certainly leads to the lower realms (*apāya*).
 * **Kammapatha vs. Light Aversion:** Merely experiencing passing thoughts of aversion does not automatically generate the heavy kamma required to determine the next rebirth (*paṭisandhi*). However, the deliberate act of ending one's life usually involves a deeply entrenched, heavy state of unwholesome volition (*kammapatha*).
 * **Evading Kamma:** Suicide cannot be used to escape past kamma. Physical suffering (such as terminal illness) is an unwholesome result (*akusalavipāka*) of past kamma. While the *citta* experiencing the pain is a resultant mind, reacting to that pain with a desire to destroy the body creates brand new, heavy unwholesome kamma.
 * **The Verdict on Higher Rebirth:** Because of the complex interactions between the 11 different causes of death and the various kammas capable of producing *paṭisandhi*, a good rebirth (*sugati*) after suicide is *theoretically* possible for a layperson, but **extraordinarily rare**.
## Summary and Conclusion
| Action Type | Primary Mental Root | Karmic Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Killing Others** | Always *Dosacitta* (Akusala) | Negative / *Apāya* |
| **Suicide (Monks)** | Defying Vinaya Rules | Bound for *Duggati* (*Āṇavītikkamantarāya*) |
| **Suicide (Average Layperson)** | Usually *Dosacitta* / Wrong View | Highly probable descent into *Apāya* |
| **Suicide (Exceptions)** | *Kusala* (Bodhisattas) / *Kiriya* (Arahats) | Exceptional / Fulfilling *Pāramī* |
### Final Advice (to Porana Yogi & Others):
Instead of seeking an escape through self-destruction—which almost guarantees a drop into the lower realms—one should actively apply wisdom to emotional pain. Use your current suffering as a vehicle for **wise attention (*yoniso manasikāra*)**, to cultivate **dispassion (*virāga*)**, spiritual urgency **(*saṃvega*)**, and deep insight **(*vipassana*)**. Bear the karmic weight of pain with a steady, enduring mind to build genuine merit.
