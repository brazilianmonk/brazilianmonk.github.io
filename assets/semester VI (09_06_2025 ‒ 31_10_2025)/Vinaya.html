      • Upassuti Sikkhāpadaṃ (in emacs)
        • background
          • The Tathāgata was residing in the Jetavana monastery near the city of Sāvatthi. During this time, the Chabbaggiya bhikkhus started fighting with well-behaved bhikkhus. The well-behaved bhikkhus said, “Friends, these Chabbaggiya monks have no shame (alajjī). They cannot be fought with.” Then Chabbaggiya monks said, “Friends, why have you said that we have no shame?” Then the well-behaved monks asked, “Where did you listen that we said so?” They said, “We were at a distance of hearing you all.” The virtuous bhikkhus thought it was lame. They criticized and blamed the Chabbaggiya monks: “How did the Chabbaggiya bhikkhus stay where the conversations of monks who are involved in fights and arguments could be heard?” The bhikkhus then informed the matter to the Tathāgata. The Tathāgata reprimanded the Chabbaggiya monks and declared the upassuti training rule considering the ten types of meanings.
        • Training rule in Pāli
          • Yo pana bhikkhu bhikkhūnaṃ bhaṇḍanajātānaṃ kalahajātānaṃ vivādāpannānaṃ upassutiṃ tiṭṭheyya - Yaṃ ime bhaṇissanti, taṃ sossāmī’ti, etadeva paccayaṃ karitvā anaññaṃ, pācittiyaṃ.
        • Description
          • When there are arguments between bhikkhus, eavesdropping on what is being discussed for the purpose of accusing them later or reminding them later about what they spoke, is not suitable for monks. The Tathāgata declared this training rule to prevent monks from such acts. It seems that this a training rule declared to settle arguments that have occur between bhikkhus and prevent them escalating. Baṇḍanajāta, kalahajāta, vivādāpanna The three terms baṇḍana, kalaha and vivāda must learnt first.
            • (1). Baṇḍanajāta – “bhaṇḍana” means conversing within one’s own group planing “If they say this, we shall say this” and so on. One who is involved in baṇḍana is called “baṇḍanajāta” (one in whom a bhaṇḍana has occurred).
            • (2). Kalahajāta - “kalaha” means fighting either verbally or physically. One who is involved in such a fight is called “kalahajāta” (one in whom a kalaha has occurred).
            • (3). Vivādajāta – “vivāda” means two groups arguing with each other saying “Our group is correct” and “No, our group is correct” and so on is called vivāda (vivādādhikaraṇa). One who is involved in (entered into) such argument is alled “vivādāpanna.”
          • Upassuti (explain upassutiṁ tiṭṭheyya: standing in a place where the sound can be heard)
            • The word, “upassuti” means standing closer to the sound (upa + suti). A person who stands in a place where the sound can be heard is said to be standing near the sound.

              • “suti” means the place one could stand and listen to the sound. The area closer to that area is called “upassuti.”
              • When that definition is taken like that “the monk is listening to the sound having stood at a place closer to the place from where one can listen to the sound.”
            • According to Sāratthadīpanī,
              • the first definition is more praiseworthy, as in the commentaries the word “upassuti” is defined as “Yattha ṭhatvā sakkā hoti, tesaṃ vacanaṃ sotuṃ” – “The palce where one can stand and listen to the sound,” and not the word “suti.”27

        • How offences are committed
          • When there are arguments between bhikkhus, eavesdropping on what is being discussed for the purpose of accusing them, will result in a pācittiya offence.
          • 4 ways
            • desire: “I will accuse him later or remind him later of what he has said.”
            • (1). A bhikkhu that goes to overhear a discussion of a monk, who is involved in a dispute (it does not need to be that the dispute is going on at that moment), will be committing dukkaṭa offences for every step he takes. When he stands in the place where the discussion can be heard, a pācittiya offence will be committed.
            • (2). When a monk who is involved in a dispute (it does not need to be that the dispute is going on at that moment), is walking in front discussing with someone else. Another monk who is coming from behind increases his speed to get closer to him. there will be a dukkaṭa offence for increasing his speed. When he reaches the area where he can listen to their talk, there will be a pācittiya offence.
            • (3). When a monk who is involved in a dispute (it does not need to be that the dispute is going on at that moment), is walking from behind discussing with someone else. Another monk who is walking ahead slows down his speed so he could get closer to the monk coming from behind. There will be a dukkaṭa offence for slowing down. When he enters the area where he can listen to their talk, there will be a pācittiya offence.
            • (4). When a monk who is involved in a dispute (it does not need to be that the dispute is going on at that moment), comes to the vicinity of another monk, who is standing, sitting or lying down, and discusses with someone else. The other monk must clear his throat and make the discussing monk know that he is there. There will be a pācittiya offence for not letting the other party know about his presence.
          • Etadeva paccayaṃ karitvā anaññaṃ pācittiyaṃ
            • The above phrase must be analyzed as following.
            • (1). Etadeva paccayaṃ karitvā … pācittiyaṃ – The offence will be committed only due to listening the conversation of others with the intention of accusing them based on what they have spoken.
            • (2). Anaññaṃ – There will be no offence if the monk listens to the conversation of others with a different intention.
            • For instance, if a monk secretly listens to the conversation of others with the intention “I shall free myself from this adhikaraṇa having listened to their words” or with the intention of solving the adhikaraṇa or with any other good intention there will be no offence.
            • Etadeva paccayaṃ karitvā anaññanti na añño koci paccayo hoti upassutiṃ tiṭṭhituṃ.
        • Non-offences
          • (1). "Imesaṃ sutvā oramissāmi viramissāmi vūpasamissāmi attānaṃ parimocessāmī"ti gacchati – a bhikkhu who goes to listen with the idea, “I will not accuse them, I will settle the argument. I will declare my innocence” and remain within the boundary of hearing, he will not be committing an offence.
        • Factors
          • (1). Upasampannatā – the bhikkhu who is involved in the argument and to whose talk one listens being higher ordained
          • (2). Codanādhippāyo – intention of accusing
          • (3). Savanaṃ – overhearing
        • Other elements related to the offence Study the following facts related to this offence.
          • (1). Citta and saññā –
            • This is a sacittaka and saññāvimokkha offence. This offence is breached only when the monk has the desire to accuse the monk later or remind him later based on what he has said.
          • (2) Āpatti and its saññā
            • This is a tika-pācittiya and tika-dukkaṭa offence. The perception involved is the upasampanna saññā (the perception that person to whose talk one listens is an upasampanna) and anupasampanna saññā (the perception that person to whose talk one listens is an anupasampanna).
          • (3). Kusalattika and vedanāttika
            • – This offence is akusala citta and dukkha vedanā.
          • (4). Vajja –
            • This is a lokavajja offence as it is only committed by akusala cittas.
          • (5). Kiriya –
            • This is a kiriya offence when the monk walks to listen to the talk. This is an akiriya offence when the monk does not clear his throat or give another verbal or physical sign when the other monk arrives at his place and starts talks.
          • (6). Samuṭṭhāna –
            • This offence can occur with kāya-citta and kāya-vācā-citta.
          • (7). Kamma –
            • kāyakamma or vacīkamma.
      • Kammappaṭibāhana Sikkhāpadaṃ
        • background
          • The Tathāgata was residing in the Jetavana monastery near the city of Sāvatthi. During this time, the Chabbaggiya bhikkhus did lots of misconducts. Then the Saṅgha did vinayakammas to those monks who did misconducts. When the vinayakammas are being done, Chabbaggiya monks protested those vinayakammas. At this time the Saṅgha gathered for a specific reason. The Chabbaggiya bhikkhus were involved in making a robe. They sent their consent to the vinayakamma through a single monk among them. Seeing that monk who came alone with the consents, the Saṅgha did a niggaha kamma to him punishing him. Subsequently, that monk returned back to the Chabbaggiya monks and informed that the Saṅgha conducted a kamma punishing him. Then the Chabbaggiya bhikkhus said that they did not give their consent to this particular kamma. Further, they mentioned that had they known that such kamma would be done for him, they would not have consented at all. Virtuous bhikkhus thought it was lame. They criticized and blamed them: “How did the Chabbaggiya monks give their consent for the karma and later criticize?” The bhikkhus then informed the matter to the Tathāgata. The Tathāgata reprimanded the Chabbaggiya bhikkhus and declared kammapaṭibhāhana training rule.
          • The Tathāgata stayed at Jetavana near Sāvatthi. The Chabbaggiya bhikkhus misbehaved, and the Saṅgha imposed vinayakammas. They protested. Later, for a robe-making issue, they sent consent via one monk. Seeing him alone, the Saṅgha punished him with a niggaha kamma. He reported back, and they denied consenting to that punishment, claiming ignorance. Other monks criticized their inconsistency and informed the Tathāgata, who rebuked them and issued the kammapaṭibhāhana rule.
        • Training rule in Pāli
          • Yo pana bhikkhu dhammikānaṃ kammānaṃ chandaṃ datvā pacchā khiyyanaDhammaṃ āpajjeyya, pācittiyaṃ.
        • Description of the training rule
          • Consenting to a righteous vinayakamma and later criticizing it is a great barrier for the unity among the Saṅgha and for the smooth execution of day-to-day work within the Saṅgha community. Therefore, the Tathāgata has declared this training rule preventing such an act.
          • There are four types of vinayakammas.
            • (1). Apalokana kamma – a vinayakamma done with the permission of the united Saṅgha having mentioned the matter (vatthuṃ kittetvā) and made the Saṅgha listen the statements such as “The Saṅgha prefers (likes) this” and so on three times. Apalokana kamma is fivefold.
              • (a). Nissāraṇa – the vinayakamma of punishing a sāmaṇera like Kaṅṭhaka who speaks bad of the Buddha, Dhamma or Saṅgha or holds a wrong view; suspending an ill-behaved sāmaṇera from the community.
              • (b). Osāraṇa – the vinayakamma of reinstating a sāmaṇera who had been punished with the nissāraṇa kamma but has corrected himself; lifting the sāmaṇera’s suspension.
              • (c). Bhaṇḍukamma – asking the permission from the Saṅgha to shave the head of a candidate who is willing to receive the sāmaṇera ordination.
              • (d). Brahmadaṇḍa – deciding that the Saṅgha should not speak with and give any admonishment to a monk who is bad-mouthed speaking to monks with rude words like Venerable Channa did.
              • (e). Kammalakkhaṇa – any vinayakamma of which the characteristic is the mere act of the vinayakamma. This was initially declared to bhikkhunīs.
                • Chabbaggiya monks doused the bhikkhunīs with water mixed with mud. Showed them their body, calves and the male organ. Talked to them with lustful words, connected bhikkhunīs with males. Then the Buddha asked bhikknunīs to do a vinayakamma so the monks who did so should not be worshiped by the bhikkhunīs. This vinayakamma can be done staying in the bhikkhunopassaya.
                • However, other agreements within the Saṅgha can also be done as an apalokana kamma and it falls under the category of kamma lakkhaṇa. The Saṅgha can allocate requisites such as robes, medicine, lodging, etc., to the monks whose robes are stolen (acchinnacīvaro), whose robes are destroyed (naṭṭhacīvaro), who is sick (gilāno), who is learned (bahussuto), one who bears the responsibilities of the Saṅgha (saṅghabhāranittharako) and so on with the agreement of the Saṅgha through an apalokana. In addition, through apalokana kammas the Saṅgha can come into agreements on consuming things such as roots, barks, leaves, sprouts, flowers, fruits of trees in a monastery, removing trees that provide shade or fruits for the construction of lodgings and so on. The Saṅgha can also agree on to allocate requisites allocated to robes and food for the purpose of lodging through an apalokana kamma. Similarly, more righteous
                • agreements can also be made withing the Saṅgha through apalokana kammas.28
                  • 28 Yaṃ pana bhagavatā bhikkhunīnaṃ ūruṃ vivaritvā...apalokanakammaṃ pañca ṭhānāni gacchati. (Kṅkh. 256.).
                  • Ūruṃ vivaritvā dassanādivatthūsūti....apiṇḍapātatthāya dinnato āvāsajagganādikaṃ tādisaṃ vā aññampi dhammikaṃ katikaṃ karontehi. (Kṅkh-abh-ṭ. 419.).
            • (2). Ñatti kamma – a vinayakamma done with the approval of the united Saṅgha with (by uttering) a single announcement (ñatti).
            • (3). Ñattidutiya kamma – a vinayakamma done with the approval of the united Saṅgha with (by uttering) one announcement (ñatti) and one proclamation (anusāvana).
            • (4). Ñatticatuttha kamma – a vinayakamma done with the approval of the united Saṅgha with (by uttering) one announcement (ñatti) and three proclamations (anusāvana).29
              • 29 Apalokanakammaṃ nāma samaggassa saṅghassa....yā tīhi ca anussāvanāhi kattabbaṃ kammaṃ ñatticatutthakammaṃ nāma. (Kṅkh. 255-7.).
          • In all the above instances, unity of the Saṅgha is decided by the arrival of all the monks within the sīma to a one assembly and being in the hatthapāsa of each other and brining the consent of the monks who are unable to attend the assembly but staying within the sīmā. However, this procedure is a bit different in the
          • bhaṇḍukamma. There are four ways the bhaṇḍu kamma can be done which was discussed under the Khandhaka Vinaya. According to the background story of the training rule, when a monk has sent the chanda together with the pārisuddhi on a uposatha day, that consent is valid for any vinayakamma done in that assembly. One does not need to specifically think as as “I am sending this chanda for this and that vinayakamma.”
        • Non-offences
          • Following is the only instance of non-offences related to this training rule.
            • (1). "Adhammena vā vaggena vā na kammārahassa vā kammaṃ kata"nti jānanto khiyyati – there is no offence for criticizing a vinayakamma after consenting knowing that it was done either unrighteously or without the unity of the Saṅgha or to a monk who did not deserve the kamma.
        • Factors
          • There are three factors to breach this training rule.
            • (1). Dhammakammatā – being a righteous vinayakamma
            • (2). Dhammakammanti saññā – perception that the vinayakamma was done righteously
            • (3). Chandaṃ datvā khiyyanaṃ – criticizing after consenting
        • Other elements related to the offence
          • Study the following facts related to this offence.
          • (1). Citta and saññā – This is a sacittaka and saññāvimokkha offence. This offence is breached only when the monk has the perception that the vinayakamma he is criticizing was done righteously.
          • (2). Āpatti – This is an ekaka-pācittiya and tika-dukkaṭa offence. The perception involved is the dhammakamma saññā (the perception that the vinayakamma one is criticizing was righteously done) and adhammakamma saññā (the perception that the vinayakamma one is criticizing was unrighteously done).
          • (3). Kusalattika and vedanāttika – This offence is akusala citta and dukkha vedanā.
          • (4). Vajja – This is a lokavajja offence as it is only committed by akusala cittas.
          • (5). Kiriya – This is a kiriya offence.
          • (6). Samuṭṭhāna – This offence can occur with kāya-citta, vācā-citta and kāya vācā-citta.
          • (7). Kamma – kāyakamma or vacīkamma.

      • Chandaṃ Adatvā Gamana Sikkhāpadaṃ
        • background
          • The Tathāgata was residing in the Jetavana monastery near the city of Sāvatthi. At this time the Saṅgha gathered for a specific reason. The Chabbaggiya bhikkhus sent their consent with one monk among them for the vinayakammas that the Saṅgha was about to do. The Saṅgha was doing a niggaha kamma to a monk. When the announcement (ñatti) was uttered and the proclamations were not finished, that monk who brought the consent stood up and walked away from the assembly having said “These monks are doing vinayakammas to each monk separately. To whom do you do the vinayakamma.” He did not give his consent for the vinayakamma. Virtuous bhikkhus thought it was lame. They criticized and blamed them: “How can a monk leave from his seat without giving his consent when an discussion to decide an issue is going on?” The bhikkhus then informed the matter to the Tathāgata. The Tathāgata reprimanded that monk and declared this training rule considering the ten types of meanings.
        • Training rule in Pāli
          • Yo pana bhikkhu saṅghe vinicchayakathāya vattamānāya chandaṃ adatvā uṭṭhāyāsanā pakkameyya, pācittiyaṃ.
        • Description of the training rule
          • A formal act of the Saṅgha needs the consent of all the monks within the sīma in which it is being performed; a vinayakamma needs the hundred percent (100%) agreement of the monks in the sīma. Either all of them have to have gathered in the assembly of the formal act (parisā), and, if every monk cannot attend, the consent of unattended monks must be sent. Monks walking away from the assembly while a righteous formal act is being performed, with the intention of invalidating it, is a great hindrance for the smooth functioning of the Saṅgha activities. The Buddha laid down this training rule preventing such misbehaviours of the monks.
          • An unfinished vinayakamma (formal act)
            • Under this training rule, an unfinished vinayakamma is defined as follows. Saṅghe vinicchayakathā nāma vatthu vā ārocitaṃ hoti avinicchitaṃ, ñatti vā ṭhapitā hoti, kammavācā vā vippakatā hoti.
            • There are three instances of an unfinished vinayakamma.
            • (1). Vatthu ārocitaṃ hoti avinicchitaṃ – According to this, in a certain adhikaraṇa such as vivādādhikaraṇa (a dispute) or anuvādādhikaraṇa (an
            • accusation), when the matters have been expressed by the complainer (or accuser) and the complained (accused) and the judge (examiner/anuvijjaka) has been appointed but the decision of the matter has not been given.
            • (2). Ñatti ṭhapitā hoti – the announcement of the vinayakamma has been made but the proclamations have not begun.
            • (3). Kammavācā vippakatā – the proclamation has begun but not ended.
            • any one of them is enough to be unfinished
          • Pakatatta, ukkhitta(ka), samānasaṃvāsaka and nānāsaṃvāsaka
            • A monk who is suspended by an ukkhepanīya kamma is called an ukkhitta(ka).
            • On the other hand, a monk who is not suspended is called a pakatatta.
              • note: there is another meaning of pakatatta: one who is not in the process of parivāsa, mānatta, etc.
            • Pakatatta monks can do their vinayakamma together. They are in communion. They share the same vinayakammas such as uposatha and pavāraṇa. It means they perform the vinayakammas together. So, they are called samānasaṃvasaka monks.
            • On the other hand, a monk who has been suspended through an ukkhepanīya vinayakamma is called an ukkhittaka monk. Pakatatta (samānasaṃvāsaka) monks are barred from doing vinayakammas with ukkhittaka monks. Therefore, ukkhittaka monks are also called nānāsaṃvāsaka monks.
            • There are two types of nānāsaṃvāsaka monks:
              • kamma nānāsaṃvāsaka
                • Kamma nānāsaṃvāsakas are the monks who have been suspended through a ukkhepanīya kamma.
              • and laddhi nānāsaṃvāsaka.
                • After the ukkhepanīaya is done to a certain monk the other monks who adopt his viewpoint and join his group are called laddhi nānāsaṃvāsaka.
              • Pakatatta monks cannot do vinayakammas with both types of nānāsaṃvāsaka monks.
            • The participation of nānāsaṃvāsaka monks in vinayakammas of samānasaṃvāsaka monks is barred. Moreover, their consent (chanda) is also not necessary. Therefore, even if a nānāsaṃvāsaka monk is in the sīma, who has not sent the chanda, while pakatatta monks are performing their vinayakamma, the formal act will not be invalid.
            • This chandaṃ adatvā gamana training rule is applicable only to pakatatta monks, not to nānāsaṃvāsaka monks.
            • ukkhittaka and ukkhittānuvattaka
            • jātaka comm. arahant in abhayagiri
        • How offences are committed
          • If a bhikkhu leaves the gathering of the vinayakamma (parisā), when the vinayakamma has not finished without giving the consent with the intention of invalidating the formal act, knowing it’s a righteous act, a dukkaṭa offence will be committed for leaving the hatthapāsa of the assembly (parisā), and a pācittiya
          • offence when as soon has he has gone beyond the hatthapāsa of the assembly (parisā).
          • Chandaṃ adatvā uṭṭhāyāsanā pakkameyyāti – "kathaṃ idaṃ kammaṃ kuppaṃ assa vaggaṃ assa na kareyyā"ti gacchati, āpatti dukkaṭassa. Parisāya hatthapāsaṃ vijahantassa āpatti dukkaṭassa. Vijahite āpatti pācittiyassa.
        • Non-offences
          • Following are the instances of non-offences related to this training rule.
          • (1). "Saṅghassa bhaṇḍanaṃ vā kalaho vā viggaho vā vivādo vā bhavissatī"ti gacchati – if left thinking “Due to this vinayakamma a argument (bhaṇḍana), a fight (kalaha), taking the opposite site (viggaha), saying possosing wrods (vivāda) will occur”, an offence will not result.
          • (2). "Saṅghabhedo vā saṅgharāji vā bhavissatī"ti gacchati – if left thinking “A a schism (saṅghabheda) will occur” or “The unity of the Saṅgha will get cracked (saṅgharāji),”an offence will not result.
          • (3). "Adhammena vā vaggena vā na kammārahassa vā kammaṃ karissatī"ti gacchati – if left thinking “The vinayakamma is done unrighteously, with disunity or to a monk who does not deserve the vinayakamma”, an offence will not result.
          • (4). Gilāno gacchati – if left due to being sick, an offence will not result.
          • (5). Gilānassa karaṇīyena gacchati – if left due to a business of a sick, an offence will not occur
          • (6). Uccārena vā passāvena vā pīḷito gacchati – If left when been afflicted by the need to pass urine or faeces, an offence will not be committed.
          • (7). "Na kammaṃ kopetukāmo puna paccāgamissāmī"ti gacchati – If left without any intention of invalidating the act thinking, “I will be back,” an offence will not result.
        • Factors
          • There are six factors to breach this training rule.
            • (1). Vinicchayakathāya pavattamānatā – the formal act being continuing; the formal act being unfinished.
            • (2). Dhammakammatā – vinayakamma being a righteous kamma
            • (3). Dhammakammasaññitā – having perception that the vinayakamma was done righteously
            • (4). Samānasīmāyaṃ ṭhitatā – being in the same sīmā
            • (5). Samānasaṃvāsakatā – being a monk of communion with the monks who are performing the vinayakamma
            • (6). Kopetukāmatāya hatthapāsavijahanaṃ – leaving the hatthapāsa without consenting with the intention of invalidating the vinayakamma
        • Other elements
          • (1). Citta and saññā – This is a sacittaka and saññāvimokkha offence. This offence is breached only when the monk has the perception that the vinayakamma is conducted righteously and has the intention of invalidating the vinayakamma.
          • (2). Āpatti – This is an ekaka-pācittiya and tika-dukkaṭa offence. The perception involved is the dhammakamma saññā (the perception that the vinayakamma one is trying to invalidate is being righteously done) and adhammakamma saññā (the perception that the vinayakamma one is trying to invalidate is being unrighteously done).
          • (3). Kusalattika and vedanāttika – This offence is akusala citta and dukkha vedanā.
          • (4). Vajja – This is a lokavajja offence as it is only committed by akusala cittas.
          • (5). Kiriya – This is a kiriyākiriya offence. Kiriya in the sense of walking away and akiriya in the sense of not giving the consent.
          • (6). Samuṭṭhāna – This offence can occur with kāya-vācā-citta.
          • (7). Kamma – kāyakamma or vacīkamma.
      • Dubbala Sikkhāpadaṃ
        • background
          • The Tathāgata was residing in the Veḷuvana monastery near the city of Rājagaha. During this time, Venerable Dabbamallaputta was preparing quarters for the Saṅgha and allocating meals. Then the Chabbaggiya monks criticized and blamed the monks, “Bhikkhus are offering what the Saṅgha received based on friendship.” The virtuous bhikkhus thought it was lame. They criticized and blamed the Chabbaggiya monks. The bhikkhus then informed the matter to the Tathāgata. For instance, if the monastery is surrounded by a fence, that surrounding fence marks its boundary. The area enclosed by the fence is considered the monastery, which is also known as the ārāma or ārāmūpacāra. If the monastery is not enclosed, the boundary is determined as two stone throws (dve leḍḍupātā) from the furthest dwelling. The area within that boundary is regarded as the ārāma or ārāmūpacāra.
            • (2). Āvasatha is the dwelling place of a monk. It can even be a place where monks dwell temporarily It may be a building located either within or outside of an ārāma.1 If the dwelling is enclosed, its boundary is its enclosure. For instance, if the dwelling is surrounded by a fence, that surrounding fence marks its boundary. The area enclosed by the fence, including the dwelling, is the āvasatha or āvasathūpacāra.
            • If the dwelling is not enclosed, its boundary is located at the distance of a winnowing basket throw (suppapāta) or a pestle throw (musalapāta) from the door of the dwelling. It is the distance where a winnowing fan or pestle will land when thrown by a woman standing at the door of the dwelling. The area within that boundary is considered the āvasatha or āvasathūpacāra.
            • “Ajjhārāma” refers to the area within the monastery (ārāma), and “ajjhāvasatha” refers to the area within the monk's dwelling (āvasatha). A monk is not allowed to collect or make someone else to collect any requisites belonging to laypeople that are found in areas outside the ārāma or āvasatha for safekeeping. Doing so will entail a pācittiya offence.
          • “Collecting or making someone else to collect for safe keeping” – “bhaṇḍāgārasīsena gahaṇaṃ vā gāhāpanaṃ”
            • Under this rule it is important to know the intention of collecting (or making someone else to collect) the requiste that has been left behind. This rule was made to prevent monks from collecting or making someone else to collect the requisites left behind by lay devotees for “safe keeping.” That means taking the property with the intention of protecting it from getting lost. Taking something with the intention of safe keeping is called “bhaṇḍāgārasīsena gahaṇaṃ.” Making someone else to collect such a requisite with the same purpose is called “bhaṇḍāgārasīsena gāhāpaṇaṃ.” Offence will occur even if the monk collects or makes someone else to collect an object that belongs to the monk’s mother (who is a lay woman) such as an ear ornament or a palm leaf for safe keeping.
            • If jātarūpa rajata (nissaggiya vatthu) are taken for one’s own benefit or have them taken, a nissaggiya pācittiya offence will occur. If taken or have them taken for the benefit of the Saṅgha, two to three bhikkhus (gaṇa) or another individual (puggala) or to build stupas (cetiya) or for constructing new things (navakamma), a dukkaṭa offence will be committed. A village was previously large, but it has become small due to families have died or left. In such case the boundry of the village must be determined by a leḍḍupāta from the vicinity of the further most house. The previous boundry is not counted even if the village was previously bounded.9
              • Leḍḍupāta (Stone Throw) and Other Measurements
                • A very important measurement found in the Vinaya is the “leḍḍupāta” (stone throw). Sometimes, young men of average strength throw a stone after stretching their arm to demonstrate their strength. The place where such a thrown stone lands is considered the leḍḍupāta. The area where the stone rolls after landing is not counted. Throwing a stone with the arm fully stretched upwards – such as when women chase away crows – or throwing underarm, as when throwing water, is not considered here.10 There are a few other traditional measurements used, as follows:
                  • (1). Suppapāta (winnowing fan throw) – the distance a winnowing fan can be thrown by a woman
                  • (2). Sammuñjanī pāta (broom throw) – the distance a broom can be thrown by a woman
                  • (3). Musala pāta (pestel throw) – the distance a pestle can be thrown by a woman
                    • These measurements are used to determine the vicinity of an unbounded house. When a woman throws a winnowing fan, broom, or pestle from within the house, the area up to the point where these objects land from the place where rainwater falls from the gutter of the eaves is considered the vicinity of the house (gharūpacāra). Among these, musala pāta is mentioned in both the Kurundi Commentary and the Mahāpaccariya Commentary. As mentioned in these two commentaries, suppapāta and musalapāta are done by a man of average strength.11
