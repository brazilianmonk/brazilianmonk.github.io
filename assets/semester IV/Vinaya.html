        • time periods
          • kāla / purebhatta - from dawn to the end of noon (noon included) - 2 parts
            • pubbaṇha – from dawn till the beginning of the ṭhitamajjhaṇha
            • ṭithamajjhaṇha / majjhaṇha – the noon; the period in which the sun is at the peak of its path in the sky
              • note: in dhammasanganī a mahathera is said to have eaten a whole meal at this time, giving the impression that it is a (short) period of time, not a brief moment
            • extra notes:
              • purebhatta has various meanings according to the context it is used but in general, purebhatta is the time before pacchābhatta - 4 types of pacchābhatta
                • from the end of the meal till the end of noon (purebhattaṃ vā pacchābhattaṃ vā kulesu cārittaṃ āpajjeyya)
                • from the end of the meal till the dawn of the following day (pacchābhattaṃ piṇḍapātapaṭikkanto)
                • vikāla (Ettha madhukapuppharaso aggipāko vā hotu ādiccapāko vā, pacchābhattaṃ na vaṭṭati)
                • after the end of noon till the sunset (Pacchābhattaṃ nāma majjhanhike vītivatte yāva atthaṅgate sūriye).

          • The materials were selected for each model considering sustainability, environment-friendliness, availability, and durability.
          • The Dhamakaraṇa was chosen to be made of the hard shell (endocarp) of coconut, taking advantage of its natural curved shape and availability.
          • The Kaṭacchuparissāvana Model 1 was chosen to be made of a single piece of cured bamboo, using a creative way of partially cutting it, allowing it to be bent in a circular shape, while retaining its strength.
          • The Kaṭacchuparissāvana Model 2 was chosen to be made of three bamboo canes cured with fire to remove natural oils that make it less durable and subject to termite and bug attacks. The natural indentations in each joint of the canes facilitates the tying of the filters bearing the water-purifying materials. The wood charcoal was directly collected from the logs to avoid contamination. The sand was collected from the natural stream and desinfected.
          • All the joints were handmade using a simple yet efficacious technique for turning reeds or grass into strong natural ropes by partially drying them and twisting them together.
          • The filters were made of two layers of natural undyed cloth according to availability.

        • ‒Instructions‒
          • How to use the Dhamakaraṇa
            • The dhamakaraṇa is a simple yet effective tool for filtrating small amounts of water for personal use making use of the force of vaccum. Place the cloth filter firmly covering its mouth, and while keeping its mouth downwards, submerge it partially in water. The water goes through the filter filling the hollow body. With a finger, close the orifice on top of the filter, and then raise the dhamakaraṇa from the water. Due to the vaccum created, the water remains in the body of the dhamakaraṇa and can be then released into a clean container either by uncovering the orifice or by turning it upside down and allowing the filtrated water to ooze out from the orifice. The filter can be easily removed and washed regularly.
          • How to use the Kaṭacchuparissāvana Model 1
            • The first model of kaṭacchuparissāvana requires little explanation and can be used by simply pouring water through it into a clean container. It can filter fairly large amounts of water, and is easy to wash and carry around. It comes with a convenient handmande rope attached to its handle.
          • How to use the Kaṭacchuparissāvana Model 2
            • The third model can be carried around in a bag with a shoulder strap. It can be prepared easily by spreading out the three canes evenly into a tripod, fastening the three filters in place, and pouring water from the top. The water goes through the filters, which act as pockets that can be filled with purifying material for the water. In this case, the three pockets are filled with sand, wood charcoal and cotton wool. A clean vessel should be placed under the filters to collect the filtrated water. The filters can be easily untied and washed, and the filtering material removed or replaced. Without the filters, the tripod can double as a stand for hanging pots, boiling water, holding poles, protecting fires, breaking winds, etc.
