      • Lesson 1: sikkhā and adhisikkhā #edited
        • what sikkhā is
          • qualities that should be repeatedly practiced, developed, and associated by anyone who is willing to garner benefits from the dispensation of the Buddha‒Sikkhitabbāti āsevitabbā. (Srd-ṭ. II. 58).
          • 3 sikkhā and their ultimate representation
            • sīlasikkhā: cetanā and viratis when practicing 5 / 10 precepts
              • Pañcaṅgadasaṅgasīlaṃ tāva sīlameva. Tañhi buddhe uppannepi anuppannepi loke pavattati. Uppanne buddhe tasmiṃ sīle buddhāpi sāvakāpi mahājanaṃ samādapenti. Anuppanne buddhe paccekabuddhā ca kammavādino ca dhammikā samaṇabrāhmaṇā cakkavattī ca mahārājāno mahābodhisattā ca samādapenti. Sāmampi paṇḍitā samaṇabrāhmaṇā samādiyanti. Te taṃ kusalaṃ dhammaṃ paripūretvā devesu ca manussesu ca sampattiṃ anubhonti. (V-a. I. 209)
            • citta sikkhā: kusalacittas, particularly those with good concentration ‒ 8 mahākusalacittas, 8 mahaggatakusalacittas
              • Kāmāvacarāni pana aṭṭha kusalacittāni, lokiyaaṭṭhasamāpatticittāni ca ekajjhaṃ katvā cittamevāti veditabbāni. Buddhuppādānuppāde cassa pavatti, samādapanaṃ samādānañca sīle vuttanayeneva veditabbaṃ. (V-a. I. 209)
            • paññā sikkhā: kammassakatañāṇa
              • "Atthi dinnaṃ, atthi yiṭṭha"nti-ādinayappavattaṃ pana kammassakatañāṇaṃ paññā, sā hi buddhe uppannepi anuppannepi loke pavattati. Uppanne buddhe tassā paññāya buddhāpi buddhasāvakāpi mahājanaṃ samādapenti. Anuppanne buddhe paccekabuddhā ca kammavādino ca dhammikā samaṇabrāhmaṇā cakkavattī ca mahārājāno mahābodhisattā ca samādapenti. Sāmampi paṇḍitā sattā samādiyanti. Tathā hi aṅkuro dasavassasahassāni mahādānaṃ adāsi. Velāmo, vessantaro, aññe ca bahū paṇḍitamanussā mahādānāni adaṃsu. Te taṃ kusalaṃ dhammaṃ paripūretvā devesu ca manussesu ca sampattiṃ anubhaviṃsu. (V-a. I. 210).
            • note: all 3 found during both buddhuppāda and abuddhuppāda periods; some wise individuals can develop them by themselves
        • what adhisikkhā is
          • adhisīlasikkhāti adhikaṃ uttamaṃ sīlanti adhisīlaṃ (V-a. I. 209); uttamanti visiṭṭhaṃ (Srd-ṭ. II. 58) (adhisīlasikkhā represents the highest forms of training of virtue)
            • adhika↔adj. plus; exceeding; more than; 2 adj. in excess #[[pāḷi vocab (3rd year)]]
            • visiṭṭha [vi + √sis + ta]↔pp. distinguished; qualified; eminent; excellent; extraordinary (sw v) #[[pāḷi vocab (3rd year)]]
          • vimativinodanī-ṭ: it includes any vivaṭṭagāmiṇī kusala ‒ any kusala virtue (even 5 precepts), consciousness with good concentration (even before jhāna), and ñāṇa (even about the buddhaguṇas or study) that serves as a supporting condition for the arising of supramundane attainments (for Nibbāna)
            • note: according to this, all pāramīs fulfilled by a bodhisatta are adhisikkhā
          • 3 adhisikkhā and their ultimate representation
            • adhisīlasikkhā
              • lokiya adhisīlasikkhā: cetanā and virati while adhering to the upasampadāsīla ‒ aka pātimokkhasaṁvarasīla
                • Pātimokkhasaṃvarasīlaṃ pana "adhisīla"nti vuccati, tañhi sūriyo viya pajjotānaṃ sineru viya pabbatānaṃ sabbalokiyasīlānaṃ adhikañceva uttamañca buddhuppādeyeva ca pavattati, na vinā buddhuppādā (V-a. I. 209).
              • lokuttara adhisīlasikkhā: virati in magga and phala cittas; most superior virtue
                • Pātimokkhasaṃvaratopi ca maggaphalasampayuttameva sīlaṃ adhisīlaṃ (V-a. I. 209)
              • note:
                • sīlasikkhā and lokiya adhisīlasikkhā suppress vītikkama defilements; lokuttara adhisīla sikkhā does samuccheda- and paṭippassaddhi-pahāna;
            • adhicittasikkhā
              • lokiya adhicittasikkhā: mahaggata jhānacittas as a basis for vipassanā
              • lokuttara adhicittasikkhā: lokuttaramagga and phala cittas; highest kusalacittas (magga)
            • adhipaññāsikkhā
              • lokiya adhipaññāsikkhā: vipassanāñāṇa that directly understands the 3 sāmaññalakkhaṇas
                • Tilakkhaṇākāraparicchedakaṃ pana vipassanāñāṇaṃ "adhipaññā"ti vuccati. Sā hi adhisīla-adhicittāni viya sīlacittānaṃ sabbalokiyapaññānaṃ adhikā ceva uttamā ca, na ca vinā buddhuppādā loke pavattati (V-a. I. 210)
              • lokuttara adhipaññāsikkhā: ñāṇa in 4 maggacittas (erradicating moha) and in phalacittas (pacifying the mental stream)
          • when are all sikkhās present in the world?
            • buddhuppāda: all sikkhās are present
            • abuddhuppāda: paccekabuddhas possess all sikkhās, but adhisikkhās are not as elaborated as those prescribed by SSB
              • the specification of offenses as sg, pc, etc is unique to SSB
              • SSB teach samatha in detail
                • 2 main ways of integrating jhāna in vipassanā
                  • pādakajjhāna: the jhāna as a basis for vipassanā
                  • sammasitajjhāna: emerging from the jhāna and using it as object of vipassanā
                    • Pātimokkhasaṃvarasīlaṃ pana "adhisīla"nti vuccati, tañhi sūriyo viya pajjotānaṃ sineru viya pabbatānaṃ sabbalokiyasīlānaṃ adhikañceva uttamañca, buddhuppādeyeva ca pavattati, na vinā buddhuppādā.                     • Adhisīlanti vuccatīti anavasesato kāyikavācasikasaṃvarabhāvato ca maggasīlassa padaṭṭhānabhāvato ca pātimokkhasaṃvarasīlaṃ adhikaṃ visiṭṭhaṃ sīlaṃ adhisīlanti vuccati.       • Lesson 7: bhāvanā vidhāna (process of meditation)
        • vidhāna↔nt. arrangement, command, performance, process #[[pāḷi vocab (3rd year)]]
        • here, meditating means taking (grasping) the kasiṇa sign into the mind
        • what is meant by nimitta at this stage (initial stage)?
          • how the earth disk appears to the eyes (or seen with the eyes or how the disk manifest to the outside)
        • how to contemplate with the kasiṇa by grasping the sign with the eyes / mind
          • eyes
            • Atiummīlayato (opened too wide) hi cakkhu kilamati, maṇḍalañca ativibhūtaṃ hoti, tenassa nimittaṃ nuppajjati.
            • Atimandaṃ ummīlayato (opened too little as an elephant gazes) maṇḍalamavibhūtaṃ hoti, cittañca līnaṃ hoti, evampi nimittaṃ nuppajjati.
            • Tasmā ādāsatale (surface of a mirror) mukhanimittadassinā viya samenākārena (in a balanced manner / in a similar manner) cakkhūni ummīletvā nimittaṃ gaṇhantena bhāvetabbaṃ: the attention should be spread throughout the entire disk
            • kālena ummīletvā kālena nimīletvā āvajjitabbaṃ (it should be adverted now with eyes opened, now with eyes closed)
          • mind
            • na vaṇṇo paccavekkhitabbo (its color should not be reflected on): vaṇṇa is paramattha and is not the focus here
            • na lakkhaṇaṃ manasikātabbaṃ (its characteristic [of hardness] should not be paid attention)
            • apica vaṇṇaṃ amuñcitvā (without letting go of the color)
              • 2 reasons it is not possible to ignore it
                • by looking at it, one necessarily has vaṇṇa as object
                • the object (attha paṭhavi paññatti) is a hard disk and that cannot be avoided
            • nissayasavaṇṇaṃ katvā (having though of them as one [tbc]): the color exists depending on the hardness, so here, the color should be "gone into sameness" (unified) with the paṭhavīdhātu and thought of as one: aruṇavaṇṇavanta paṭhavi dhātu (dawn-coloured hardness)
              • samānagatika↔gone into sameness, become one, unify
            • ussadavasena (?) paṇṇattidhamme cittaṃ paṭṭhapetvā (having established the mind on the concept by means of its prominency) manasi kātabbaṃ (should be attended to).
            • masikātabbaṁ: contemplation must be done repeatedly thinking “paṭhavi, paṭhavi”, normally mentally, but can also be done verbally
            • yāva uggahanimittaṃ nuppajjati, tāva kālasatampi kālasahassampi tato bhiyyopi eteneva nayena bhāvetabbaṃ (until the uggahanimitta arises, one should cultivate in this manner even a hundred, a thousand, or even more times
        • if the eyes are too opened, the hardness and the dawn colour of the disk will be prominent to the mind. Then the nimitta is said to be too obvious – “maṇḍalañca ativibhūtaṃ hoti”. Therefore, when looking at the kasiṇa sign having opened the eyes too widely, the acquired sign (uggaha nimitta) will not arise.
        • 4 meanings of paṭhavī
          • paramattha paṭhavī: hardness; paṭhavīdhātu; the 1st of the 28 rūpas; has neither shape nor color; is not extended; functions by being a base / support for other ass. rūpas
          • attha (paṭhavī) paññatti: the concept (cittanimitta) of a solid extended material body constituted of multiple rūpakalāpas with prominent paṭhavīdhātu taken as a single object based on its pavatti ākāra, and that has shape, and often color (when not?); this is the object of paṭhavīkasiṇa meditation #q🙋🏼‍♂

    • Suttavibhanga (Analysis of Rules)
      • 1. Suttavibhanga (Analysis of Rules)
        • Approximate Date Written: Traditionally dated to the First Buddhist Council (circa 483 BCE) shortly after the Buddha’s death, though written down around the 1st century BCE in Sri Lanka during the reign of King Vattagamani Abhaya. Scholars suggest its core content predates the separation of Buddhist schools (circa 4th century BCE), with later redactions.
        • Author: Traditionally attributed to the Buddha, with recitation and compilation credited to Upali, one of the Buddha’s chief disciples, at the First Buddhist Council. Upali was known for his expertise in monastic discipline.
          • Brief Description of Author: Upali was a former barber who became a monk and one of the Buddha’s ten principal disciples. He was renowned for his mastery of the Vinaya and recited it at the First Council, ensuring its preservation.
        • Brief Description of Content: The Suttavibhanga provides a detailed commentary on the Patimokkha, the core list of monastic rules (227 for monks, 311 for nuns in the Theravada tradition). It is divided into two sections:
          • Bhikkhu-vibhanga: Rules for monks, covering offenses from severe (e.g., expulsion for sexual intercourse) to minor (e.g., confession for disrespect).
          • Bhikkhuni-vibhanga: Rules for nuns, expanding on the monks’ rules with additional regulations specific to female monastics. Each rule includes its origin story (how the Buddha established it) and prescribed penalties.
    • Khandhaka (Sections)
      • 2. Khandhaka (Sections)
        • Approximate Date Written: Likely compiled alongside the Suttavibhanga, with oral origins at the First Council (circa 483 BCE) and written down in the 1st century BCE. Some sections may reflect later monastic developments (circa 3rd–1st century BCE).
        • Author: Attributed to the Buddha, with compilation by his early followers, possibly including Upali and other senior monks. No single author is identified, as it evolved through communal recitation.
          • Brief Description of Author: The collective authorship reflects the early Sangha’s efforts to preserve the Buddha’s teachings. Upali’s influence is probable, given his role in the Vinaya’s transmission.
        • Brief Description of Content: The Khandhaka consists of 22 chapters divided into two sub-books:
          • Mahavagga (Great Section): 10 chapters covering admission to the Sangha, the Uposatha ceremony (recitation of rules), rules for the rainy season retreat, and daily monastic life (e.g., food, clothing, lodging). It includes narratives of the Buddha’s life and the Sangha’s early history.
          • Cullavagga (Smaller Section): 12 chapters detailing procedures for handling disputes, disciplinary actions (e.g., suspension), and specific instructions for nuns. It also recounts significant events, like the First and Second Buddhist Councils.
    • Parivara (Appendix)
      • 3. Parivara (Appendix)
        • Approximate Date Written: Considered the latest addition, likely composed after the 1st century BCE, possibly as late as the 4th century CE in Sri Lanka. It postdates the Fourth Buddhist Council (circa 29 BCE) and reflects Theravada-specific refinements.
        • Author: Unknown; traditionally attributed to the Theravada monastic community in Sri Lanka, possibly under the influence of later scholars like Buddhaghosa (5th century CE), though he is not the author.
          • Brief Description of Author: The anonymous compilers were likely Sri Lankan monks who sought to systematize and summarize the Vinaya for practical use, building on earlier oral traditions.
        • Brief Description of Content: The Parivara is a supplementary text unique to the Theravada tradition, serving as a digest and analysis of the rules in the Suttavibhanga and Khandhaka. It organizes the rules into categories, provides question-and-answer formats, and offers mnemonic aids for monks to understand and apply the Vinaya. It is less narrative-driven and more analytical.
    • General Notes
      • Authorship Context: While the Buddha is traditionally credited with establishing the rules, the texts were orally transmitted by his disciples (notably Upali) and written down centuries later. The Vinaya evolved as the Sangha grew, with rules added reactively to specific incidents.
      • Historical Development: The Suttavibhanga and Khandhaka form the older core, likely standardized by the 3rd century BCE, while the Parivara is a later Theravada-specific addition. The Pali Canon, including the Vinaya Pitaka, was committed to writing in Sri Lanka around 29 BCE, though its content reflects earlier traditions.
      • Purpose: Together, these books regulate monastic conduct, ensure harmony within the Sangha, and maintain the ethical integrity of the Buddhist community, reflecting the Buddha’s emphasis on "Dhamma-Vinaya" (Doctrine and Discipline).
    • Commentaries (Atthakatha)
      • The primary commentary on the Vinaya Pitaka is the Samantapasadika, composed in the 5th century CE. Commentaries were originally part of an older Sinhala tradition but were translated and reworked into Pali by key figures like Buddhaghosa.
      • Samantapasadika (The Complete Trustworthy Explanation)
        • Approximate Date Written: Circa 430–450 CE, during the reign of King Mahanama in Sri Lanka.
        • Author: Buddhaghosa, a prolific Indian monk and scholar who traveled to Sri Lanka.
          • Brief Description of Author: Buddhaghosa (meaning "Voice of the Buddha") was a 5th-century Theravada scholar from India (possibly Magadha). He translated and systematized Sinhala commentaries into Pali, producing authoritative works still revered today. He resided at the Mahavihara in Anuradhapura.
        • Content: A comprehensive commentary on the entire Vinaya Pitaka:
          • Introduction (Nidana): Historical context, including the Buddha’s life and the First Council.
          • Suttavibhanga: Explains each rule’s background, exceptions, and legalistic details (e.g., what constitutes a violation).
          • Khandhaka: Clarifies procedures (e.g., ordination, Uposatha), often citing Sinhala sources.
          • Parivara: Less detailed here, as it’s already a summary, but integrates it into the broader framework.
        • Significance: The Samantapasadika bridges the canonical text and practical application, resolving ambiguities and standardizing Theravada monastic practice.
      • 1. Mahā Aṭṭhakathā (The Great Commentary)
        • Approximate Date: Originated shortly after the First Buddhist Council (circa 483 BCE) as an oral tradition, with written forms likely emerging between the 3rd century BCE and 1st century BCE in Sri Lanka. It was part of the Sinhala commentarial tradition committed to writing around the 1st century BCE alongside the Pali Canon.
        • Author: No single author; traditionally considered a collective work of the early Sangha, particularly the monks of the Mahāvihāra. It is said to have been based on explanations of the Buddha’s teachings recited by Upali and others at the First Council, though this is a traditional attribution rather than a historically verified fact.
          • Brief Description of Author: The Mahāvihāra monks were the custodians of Theravada orthodoxy in ancient Sri Lanka, tasked with preserving the Buddha’s teachings in Pali and Sinhala after the Canon was brought to the island by Mahinda (circa 3rd century BCE).
        • Content: The Mahā Aṭṭhakathā was the foundational Sinhala commentary on the entire Pali Canon (Vinaya, Sutta, and Abhidhamma Pitakas). It provided detailed exegesis of the scriptures, including explanations of rules, suttas, and philosophical terms, as well as historical narratives about the Buddha and the early Sangha. It served as a primary source for Buddhaghosa’s later works (e.g., Samantapāsādikā), and parts of it are preserved in his commentaries. It also contained material later used in chronicles like the Mahāvaṃsa and Dīpavaṃsa. The text was superseded by Buddhaghosa’s Pali commentaries and is no longer extant in its entirety.

        • Approximate Date: Likely composed between the 3rd century BCE and 1st century CE, though precise dating is uncertain due to its loss. It is traditionally dated to the early centuries of the Sinhala commentarial tradition, possibly overlapping with or following the Mahā Aṭṭhakathā.
        • Author: Unknown; attributed to the Mahāvihāra monks as a collective effort, possibly a specialized group within the community focusing on specific texts or interpretations.
          • Brief Description of Author: The Mahāvihāra monks were a scholarly lineage dedicated to preserving and interpreting the Theravada Canon, emphasizing purity of doctrine over sectarian deviations.
        • Content: The Mahāpaccariya is thought to have been a detailed commentary, possibly focusing on the Vinaya or Sutta Pitaka, though its exact scope is unclear. Its name suggests it was a significant ("great") work, perhaps addressing individual (pacceka) rules or suttas with extensive analysis. It is referenced in later Pali commentaries (e.g., by Buddhaghosa), indicating it was a key source, but it was eventually supplanted and lost. Some scholars suggest it may have overlapped with the Mahā Aṭṭhakathā in content but differed in emphasis or style.
      • 3. Kurundi (The Kurundi Commentary)
        • Approximate Date: Likely composed between the 3rd century BCE and 1st century CE, part of the early Sinhala tradition written down by the 1st century BCE.
        • Author: Unknown; traditionally linked to the Mahāvihāra monks, possibly named after a location (Kurunda) or a specific elder who contributed to it.
          • Brief Description of Author: The Mahāvihāra monks, as with other early commentaries, were a scholarly community preserving oral traditions in Sinhala before their translation into Pali.
        • Content: The Kurundi is believed to have been a commentary primarily on the Sutta Pitaka, though some sources suggest it included Vinaya material. It is frequently cited by Buddhaghosa (e.g., in the Samantapāsādikā and Visuddhimagga), indicating its authority. Its name may derive from a monastery or region (Kurunda in Sri Lanka), and it likely provided detailed explanations of suttas, including narrative expansions and doctrinal clarifications. Like other Sinhala commentaries, it was replaced by Buddhaghosa’s works and is no longer extant.
      • 4. Andhaka (The Andhra Commentary)
        • Approximate Date: Estimated between the 3rd century BCE and 1st century CE, though its origins may tie to the Andhra region of India before being integrated into the Sinhala tradition in Sri Lanka.
        • Author: Unknown; possibly composed by monks from the Andhra region (southern India) or adopted by the Mahāvihāra from an earlier Indian source.
          • Brief Description of Author: If linked to Andhra, the authors could have been early Theravada monks from mainland India who influenced the Sri Lankan tradition before the Mahāvihāra standardized the commentaries.
        • Content: The Andhaka is less well-documented but is referenced by Buddhaghosa, suggesting it was an authoritative commentary on the Pali Canon, possibly the Sutta or Abhidhamma Pitaka. Its name implies a connection to the Andhra country, hinting at a regional tradition that was transmitted to Sri Lanka. It likely offered alternative interpretations or supplementary material to the Mahā Aṭṭhakathā, but it too was lost after Buddhaghosa’s recension into Pali.

          • Linguistic analysis of Pali terms in the Vinaya.
