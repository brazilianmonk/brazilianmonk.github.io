  • The relationship between the object of representation and the mental content
  • The object of representation is an ultimate reality and it can be considered either as an ultimate reality or a paññatti. For example, take the five aggregates. They are a collection of mental and physical realities. When considering about the five aggregates, usually people take it be a “human” having collectively taken all the aggregates at once. The “idea of human” is the way the five aggregates were taken by the mind. That idea is the “mental content.” The idea of human can be intellectually dissected into it components. Therefore, it is an hīnattha. This is considering a group of paramatthas as a hīnattha. A yogi who practices vipassanā, takes the five aggregates as five aggregates as five aggregates, not as a human. That is the act of taking ultimate realities as ultimate realities.
  • Therefore, there are two types of mental content:
    • (1). Paññatti mental content (paññatti citta nimitta) – considering an existing reality or realities as a non-existing entity (e.g., idea of a human)
    • (2). Paramattha mental content (paramattha citta nimitta) – considering an existing reality or realities as existing realities (e.g., idea of feeling, perception etc.)
  • One should not misunderstand the “paramattha mental content” as another ultimate reality. It is the consideration of an ultimate reality as an ultimate reality. Paññattis are the paññatti citta nimitta. We do not discuss about the paramattha citta nimitta separately in Dhamma.1
  • In sentences such as “Rūpaṃ attato samanupassati” are examples of the consciousness considering ultimate realities as a paññati (concept). In the above piece of teaching “rūpa” (matter) indicates the “object of reference” which is an ultimate reality. The term “attato” (as self) indicates the “mental content” (citta nimitta) which is an hīnattha (concept/paññatti). That mental content about a self is also called “atta chāyā” (reflection (reflected image) of a self). However, according to Dhamma, it is also correct to say that the object of the mind, at that time, is a paramattha (the matter) or a paññatti (the atta chāyā).2
  • An object of reference can be taken by the mind in various ways. There are many ways an object of reference can be taken. An object of reference can be taken as a paramattha. It can also be taken as a paññatti. Such an object can be taken as a “pleasant thing” (subha), “unpleasant thing” (asubha) or “neutral thing” (majjhatta). Moreover, an object of reference can be taken as a “permanent thing” (nicca), “impermanent thing” (anicca), “thing which begets suffering” (dukkha), “thing which begets bliss” (sukha), “self” (atta) or “non-self thing” (anatta). Consider the following examples
  • (1). Sometimes, the consciousness takes object of reference (which is a paramattha) as a paramattha. Ten viññāṇas, two sampaṭicchanas, three santīraṇas and eight mahāvipākas take paramattha objects as paramattha.
    • C (UR) ⇒ UR
  • (2). Sometimes, akusala cittas take object of reference as paramatthas but either with attachment, anger (disliking) or delusion. Attaching to a very beautiful scenery and disliking the heat are good examples for this type of cognition.
    • Dosamūlacitta (UR) ⇒ UR (disliking heat)
  • (3). Mahākusala and mahākiriya cittas, too, can take paramattha objects as paramatthas. On some (correction) occasions, they understand the universal characteristics and consider them either as impermanent, suffering or non-self.
  • (4). Sometimes, unwholesome consciousnesses can take paramattha objects either as a being who has been living for a long time or permanent (hīnattha). Moreover, bunches or realities can be taken as things such as chair, table, house etc., (hīnatthas) At that time the mind either attaches to the object, dislike it or is deluded about it.
  • (5). Even the mahākusala and mahāvipāka cittasi can take paramattha objects as a living being and spread loving kindness towards that being. Sometimes, bunches of realities can be taken as houses, chairs, tables, etc., and perform wholesome acts such as donating them.

  • However, when a paramattha is known as a paramattha, it must be said, the actual object of the consciousness is always the object of reference. Paramattha citta nimitta (paramattha mental content) is never said to be the object of the consciousness. However, when the mind is focusing on a suddha paññatti nimitta like the paṭibhāga nimitta, which does not have an object of reference, the object of the consciousness is the paññatti citta nimitta (paññatti). When the there is an object of reference, the actual object of the consciousness can either be the “object of reference” or the “mental content.”4 If there is no object of reference, the object will be the mental content. As the mental content is a must in every consciousness, according to Theravada, no citta arises without an object.5

  • Kamma, Object of Reference and the Mental Content
    • Under the law of kamma, what matters the most is not the mental content but the object of reference. If we are to perform a certain wholesome or unwholesome act towards a person, the kamma is accumulated not mainly on the nature of the mental content, but according to the nature of the object of reference. Let’s take an example story. One of your friends tells you a story about a monk he knows, whom you have not met before. While listening to the story, an idea appears in your mind about the particular monk. That idea (mental content) is constructed with your imagination listening to the story, and your past experiences about figures of monks greatly influence the nature of the content. That content most probably does not correspond with the true figure of the monk story refers to. However, according to Theravada, at that time, the object of your mind is ‘the monk about whom the story is,’ it means, the person your content refers to, that is to say, the object of reference – monk about whom the story is.
    • How do we prove this notion within the Tradition? For that first we extend our story. Now during the discussion, your friend talks bad about that monk. Listening to that, you, too, joins him and accuses the monk with harsh words (upavadati). Unfortunately, that monk happens to be an Arahant. What will be the result? You commit the unwholesome deed called ariyūpavāda (accusation of a noble being). One can argue that the content of your mind did not fit with the actual nature of that monk, so that you are not liable to bad results of the immoral deed. According to Theravada, content of the mind is not the actual object of the mind. The real object of the mind is the object of reference, it means, according to the example story, it is the monk about whom the story is. In the above incident, you have accumulated the kamma based on the nature of the object of reference. Your mental content was about the unvirtuous puthujjana monk who does improper acts. However, that monk you referred to happen to be an Arahant. The kamma was accumulated not based on the mental content but based on the object of reference.
    • Evidence can be fetched from the literature to strengthen this argument. Once a sister of the Buddha Sikhī who was a nun, saw a lump of phlegm on the compound of a Pagoda. She got angry and scolded “Which prostitute has split phlegm on this sacred ground?”. Unfortunately for her, that lump of phlegm was of an Arahant bhikkhuṇī. Whatever the content she had, her mind was directed towards the person who actually split the phlegm. Therefore, the object of her mind was the person out of whom phlegm came. Hence, she accumulated the kamma of ariyūpavāda.
    • In the same manner, when we pay respect to the Buddha, we imagine his appearance based on the Buddha statues we have seen or information we have heard about him. Those statues never look like the actual Tathāgata. Nevertheless, when we pay homage to him, despite the nature of the content we have in our mind, our mind is directed to the Gotama Buddha who actually lived. So, we accumulate the merit of paying respect to the Buddha. Does such an act of paying respect to the Buddha by us equals with the act of paying homage to the living Buddha? It depends upon the mindset of the worshiper. If a person’s level of faith, happiness and wisdom while paying homage to the deceased Buddha is as same as while he pays homage to the living Buddha, the strength of the accumulated merit is equal. This notion is thus mentioned in the Vimānavatthupāḷi.
