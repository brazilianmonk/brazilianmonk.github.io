In the evening and morning, there should be two sessions of practicing loving-kindness (mettā), two recitations of protection (Paritta), two sessions of contemplating the unattractive nature of the body (asubha), two sessions of mindfulness of death (maraṇānussati), and attention to the eight important reminders (aṭṭhamahāsaṃvegavatthu). The eight important reminders are: birth, old age, illness, death, and the suffering in the four unhappy realms. Alternatively, the first four are birth, old age, illness, and death, followed by the suffering in the unhappy realm, the suffering resulting from past actions, the suffering anticipated in future lives, and the suffering arising from the struggle to find food in the present."

        • Assignment - fifteen great qualities in mettāsutta #[[khuddaka exam]]
          • by Ariyañāṇa Bhikkhu
          • sakko
            • One is able (sakko) when one fulfills the second and fourth factors of striving, namely appābādho appātaṅko and āraddhavīriyo
          • uju & suhuju
            • One is considered upright (uju) or very upright (suhuju) according to four methods:
              • uju: occasional uprightness or while one is young suhuju: habitual uprightness
              • uju: asaṭha (not showing qualities one does not possess) suhuju: amāyāvī (not hiding one's faults)
              • uju: abandoning bodily and verbal crookedness suhuju: abandoning mental crookedness
              • uju: not showing the virtues that one does not have (asaṭha) suhuju: not consenting to gains accrued due to non-existent virtues
          • suvaco
            • One is easy to admonish (suvaco) when one does not show anger, displeasure, give excuses or starts criticizing another when being admonished, but listens respectfully, like Sāriputta Mahāthera. It is of two kinds as āmisahetusuvaco and dhammagāravahetusuvaco. The second is meant here
          • mudu
            • One is gentle (mudu) when one acts joyfully and kindly while performing physical and verbal actions, avoiding conflict and opposition, and cultivating harmony, being pleasant and welcoming like a safe harbour
          • anatimānī
            • One if free from superiority conceit (atimāna) due to birth, clan, and so on, like a caṇḍala boy, like a viper without fangs, like a bull without horns or like a floor mat
          • santussako
            • One is content (santussako) when possessed of the twelve types of santosa. Alternatively one is content with what is one's own (sakena tussako), with what is available (santena tussako) and content evenly (samena tussako)
          • subharo
            • One is easy to support (subharo) when one is content with what is offered being it little or much, and having made use of it happily, go about doing their own work
          • appakicco
            • One has few duties (appakicco) when one is not engaged in time consuming duties such as those in relation to building monasteries, managing Saṅgha properties, administrating novices and workers. One is free from the ten impediments:
              • "āvāso ca kulaṃ lābho, gaṇo kammañca pañcamaṃ, addhānaṃ ñāti ābādho, gantho iddhīti te dasāti"
          • sallahukavuttī
            • One has a light livelihood (sallahukavuttī) when one lives depending on few possessions, like a bird, such as the aṭṭhaparikkhāra:
              • "ticīvarañca pattañca vāsi suci ca bandhanaṁ parissāvanena aṭṭhete yuttayogassa bhikkhuno"
          • santindriyo
            • One has calm faculties (santindriyo or anuddhatindriyo) when one restrains lust and other defilements from arising when encountering pleasant or unpleasant objects
          • nipako
            • One is wise (nipako) when one is possessed of wisdom in protecting virtues, finding requisites, in the sattasappāya, or alternatively in the four sampajañña, namely, that of purpose, of suitability, of domain and of non-delusion
          • appagabbho
            • One is not impudent (appagabbho) when free of bodily, verbal and mental impudence such as disrespectful behavior towards teachers, in public places, or towards the Saṅgha.
          • kulesu ananugiddho
            • One is without attachment to families (kulesu ananugiddho) when one approaches suitable families drawing back body and mind, always as a newcomer, without attachment, like the moon that comes and goes without sorrowing with them (na sahasokī), and without rejoicing with them (na sahanandī)
          • na ca khuddamācare kiñci yena viññū pare upavadeyyuṁ
            • One should not perform even small misdeeds of body, speech or mind, for which one would experience the present danger (sandiṭṭhika ādīnava) of being censured by the wise. One should not disregard any evil as little, as it is said in Dhp 121:
              • "māvamaññetha pāpassa, na mantaṃ āgamissati, udabindunipātena, udakumbhopi pūrati, bālo pūrati pāpassa, thokaṃ thokampi ācinaṃ"

            • piṇḍapātasantosa
            • senāsanasantosa

            • note:
              • the first three should be practiced as follows:
                • 1. a monk is content
                • 2. he praises contentment
                • 3. he does not engage in a wrong search (anesanā)

                • 5. he makes use of them without infatuation, seeing the danger in them and understanding the escape from them
                • 6. he does not extol himself or disparage others because of them

          • 11.1.Aviññāṇaka (insentient or inanimate) ratana is the wheel gem, the jewel gem, gold, silver, diamond or anything else devoid of faculties born out of kamma. On the other hand, saviññāṇaka (sentient or animate) ratana is the hatthi ratana, pariṇāyaka ratana, gahapati ratana, or anything else endowed with sense faculties. Among these two, the sentient gem is superior since insentient gems are used to adorn the sentient, such as the elephant gem.
          • 11.2.Saviññāṇaka (sentient) ratana is also of two kinds: Tiracchānagataratana (animal gems) and manussa-ratana (human gems). The later one is superior because the animal gems are used as conveyances or vehicles for human gems.
          • 11.3.Manussa ratana (human gems) is also of two kinds: Itthiratana (female gems) and purisaratana (male gems). The later one is superior since female gems provide service to male gem.
          • 11.4.Purisa ratana (male gems) is also of two kinds: Agārika ratana (home dwelling gems) and Anagārika ratana (homeless gems). The later one is superior since even the wheel-turning monarch, kings and so forth pay homage to homeless gems who possess excellent qualities such as good behavior, etc., attend on them and by that they achieve celestial and human prosperity and the Nibbāna.
          • 11.5.Anagārika ratana (homeless gems) is also of two kinds: Puthujjana ratana (worldling gems) and Ariya ratana (noble gems). Out of the two, ariya ratana is superior to puthujjana ratana.
          • 11.6.Ariya ratana (the gem of the noble ones), too, is of two kinds: Sekha-ratana (trainee gems) and Asekha-ratana (adept, arahant, one who does not require any further training).
          • 11.7.Asekha ratanam (the gem of Arahant), too, is of two kinds: Sukkavipassaka (dry-insight arahants) and Samathayānika (those who use serenity as their vehicle). Out of the two, Samathayānika ratana is superior to Sukkavipassaka ratana.
          • 11.8.Samathayānika ratana (those who use serenity as their vehicle) is also of two kinds: Sāvakapāramippatta (those who have reached the perfection of a disciple) and Sāvakapāramī-appatta (those who have not reached it). The first one is superior because of the greatness of their excellent qualities.

      • summary of the story of Brahmin Saṅkha and his son, Susīma
        • Brahmin Saṅkha's son, Susīma, seeks to learn a craft in Bārāṇasī. Susīma learns in a few months what normally took 12 years, but seeks the craft's end, which his teacher doesn't know, referring him to the Paccekabuddhas. He goes to Isipatana to ask paccekabuddhas, who ordain him and teach him conduct. Susīma attains pacceka enlightenment, attains to gain and renown but has a short life. His father, Saṅkha, seeks him in Bārāṇasī and learns about Susīma's enlightenment and passing. Saṅkha grieves and makes pūjā at Susīma's cetiya. The Blessed One connects the story with the present, revealing he was Saṅkha. The act of generosity in the past leads to grand offerings. The Dhamma talk concludes with a verse on renouncing slight happiness for greater happiness. merits done by Saṅkha at Susīma's cetiya and the kammic results they produced in the context of ratanasutta: 1. he plucked out the weeds: as a consequence the stumps and thorns along the eight-yojana journey were removed and the ground was made smooth and clean 2. he brought sand in his upper garment and scattered it over the terrace: as a consequence sand was scattered over the eight-yojana journey 3. he made an offering of woodland flowers: as a consequence, over the nine-yojana journey including land and water, a carpet was made with various flowers 4. he moistened the ground with water from his pot: as a consequence, a lotus-leaf rain fell on Vesālī 5. he set up a banner and tied a parasol there: as a consequence, banners were set up and row upon row of parasols were raised aloft right up to the Akaniṭṭha heaven verse: "mattāsukhapariccāgā, passe ce vipulaṃ sukhaṃ, caje mattāsukhaṃ dhīro, sampassaṃ vipulaṃ sukhaṃ“ If by renouncing a slight happiness one sees a greater happiness ahead, the wise person should give up the slight happiness, considering the greater happiness ahead (Dhp 290) Summary: A Brahmin's son, Susīma, seeks knowledge in Bārāṇasī, attains enlightenment as a paccekabuddha, and his father, Saṅkha, later learns of his son's fate, makes pūjās at his cetiya (weeds, sand, flowers, water, banner and parasol), The Buddha explains He was Saṅkha and shows the results of the meritorious deeds ending with a verse
