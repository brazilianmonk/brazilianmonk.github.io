On Walking and Sitting Still

Long ago, in a quiet forest monastery, an old teacher was asked by a visitor: "What is the secret of your practice?" The teacher smiled and said, "When I walk, I walk. When I sit, I sit." The visitor, puzzled, said, "But I also walk and sit." The teacher replied, "When you walk, you are already thinking of sitting. When you sit, you are already thinking of walking."

I. The Path Through the Trees

Every morning before the sun cleared the ridge, the monks would rise and walk the long path that circled the monastery grounds. It was not a walk toward anywhere in particular — the path began and ended at the same worn stone — but each step was said to be complete in itself. The gravel crunched, the birds began their small arguments in the canopy, and the mist lifted slowly off the lower fields like a held breath finally released.

A young novice once asked why they walked in circles instead of out into the wider world. The old teacher only pointed to the ground beneath their sandals. "The world is here too," he said. "You do not need to travel to arrive."

"Walking, I only walk. Sitting, I only sit. Above all, I don't wobble."

II. Sitting Beneath the Bodhi Tree

By midday the walking would end, and the monks would settle beneath the old fig tree at the center of the courtyard. There was no signal, no bell — simply an understanding that passed silently between them, the way a flock of birds turns together in the sky without a single leader calling the change. Legs folded, spines straightened gently like reeds finding their own balance, and the breath slowed until it was hardly a thing that needed doing at all.

Sitting, the teacher would say, is not the opposite of walking. It is what walking is made of, if you look closely enough — a thousand tiny stillnesses stacked one after another, each foot pausing on the earth before it lifts to fall again.

III. The Lesson of the Candle

One evening, a storm rolled in from the west and knocked out the lanterns along the corridor. In the dark, a young monk fumbled for a match and lit a single candle, cupping the flame with both hands as he carried it down the hall. "You walk so carefully now," the teacher observed from his doorway. "Why?" The young monk said, "Because if I rush, the flame goes out." The teacher nodded. "That is all walking ever was."

IV. Returning to the Beginning

Seasons turned. Novices became teachers themselves, and the path around the monastery grew a little deeper with each passing year of footsteps, the way a river carves its bed not through force but through patient repetition. Visitors would sometimes ask what had changed in all that time. Very little, the monks would answer — we still walk when we walk, and sit when we sit.

And if you have read this far, you have already done something similar: one line after another, unhurried, arriving here not because you leapt to the bottom of the page, but because you walked it, step by step — or, perhaps, because a small monk walked it for you.

"The journey is not carried, it is walked — even when the walking is only your eyes moving down a page."
