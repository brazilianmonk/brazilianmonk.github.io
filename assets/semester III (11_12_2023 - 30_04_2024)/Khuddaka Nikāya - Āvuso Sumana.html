        • background
          • While the Buddha was staying in the bamboo grove (Veḷuvana), the squirrel sanctuary in Rājagaha city, venerable Lakkhaṇa and venerable Mahāmoggallāna were staying on Gijjhakūṭa mountain. At one time, venerable Mahāmoggallāna got dressed in the morning, took the bowl and outer robe, and approached venerable Lakkhaṇa, saying, 'Friend, shall we enter Rājagaha city for alms-food?' Venerable Lakkhaṇa replied, 'Yes, friend.' While climbing down from Gijjhakūṭa mountain at a certain place, venerable Mahāmoggallāna smiled. After seeing the smile, venerable Lakkhaṇa asked, 'Friend Moggallāna, what is the reason? What is the cause for your displaying a smile?' Mahāmoggallāna responded, 'This is not the proper time to ask this question, friend Lakkhaṇa. Please ask me in front of the Buddha.' After finishing the alms-round and the meal, both of them approached the Buddha, paid respect, and sat on one side. Venerable Lakkhaṇa, who was sitting there, asked venerable Mahāmoggallāna the same question. Venerable Mahāmoggallāna responded, 'While climbing down Gijjhakūṭa mountain, I saw a skeleton (a chain of bones) moving in the sky. Vultures, crows, and hawks, chasing it, were pecking at it between ribs, stabbing it, tearing it apart while the skeleton peta was crying out loud. Friend Lakkhaṇa, the thought occurred to me: "It was amazing indeed, it was marvelous indeed that such a being exists, such a demon comes into being, and a being can possess such a form. The Buddha then addressed the monks, saying, 'Oh, monks, disciples who have such eyes and knowledge know, see, and witness such a being. I had seen this being (peta) before, but I did not tell. If I told about it, others would not have believed me. It would have been harmful and caused suffering for them for a long time.'" The cause: the Buddha explained that this being used to be a cow butcher (goghātaka)1 in Rājagaha city. As a result of that karma, he underwent boiling in hell for many years, hundreds of years, and even thousands of years. Due to the residue effects of this kmma action2 , he was experiencing suffering in his present form.

          • in Rājagaha to King Bimbisāra on the second day of the great offering made by the king to the Buddha, because he wished to understand and see the situation of the petas
        • 92 kappas ago, there was a city named Kāsi where a king named Jayasena and his queen Sirimā dwelt; the queen conceived the bodhisatta Phussa; after enlightenment the king, considering the Buddha and the Saṅgha his, attended them without allowing others to do so. The Buddha's three half-brothers, wishing to extend such benefits to others, employed a plan, creating a mock uprising on the kingdom's border. The king sent them, and after returning the king offered them a boon. They asked to attend upon the Buddha, and after much bargain, were given 3 months to do so. Some individuals got to make offerings, but some others, being envious, destroyed some of the offerings. The generous ones were reborn in the heavens while the envious ones in hell for for 92 kappas. During Kassapa Buddha's time, the envious ones became petas and after inquiring from Him how could they attain prosperity were told that they could only acquire it during Buddha Gotama's time.
        • When one Buddhantara had passed away, our Buddha was born in the world. The three king’s sons, along with 1,000 people, died from the celestial world and were reborn into a Brahmin family. Gradually, they went forth as hermits. They were three dreadlocked ascetics (jaṭila) in Gayāsīsa. The welfare officer was reborn as King Bimbisāra. The storekeeper was reborn as the millionaire Visākha. His wife was reborn as a millionaire's daughter named Dhammadinnā. The rest of the company became the retinues of King Bimbisāra. The Blessed One, having attained enlightenment in the world, after seven weeks, journeyed to the city of Bārāṇasī in sequence, and set the Wheel of Dhamma in motion. He made Pañcavaggiya attained enlightenment and trained the three dreadlocked ascetics together with 1,000 retinues2 , and then went to the Rājagaha city. There, he facilitated King Bimbisāra in attaining Sotāpattiphala upon his arrival, along with eleven nahuta Brahmins and householders in Magadha. Subsequently, the Buddha accepted the king’s invitation for the following day's meal.
          • During the time of the Kassapa Buddha, stingy and envious individuals were reborn as hungry ghosts. They sought advice from the Buddha on attaining prosperity, and were told that in the future, during the time of the Buddha Gotama and King Bimbisāra, they would have the opportunity to attain it. Later, the three royal sons and others were reborn and played significant roles in the Buddha's journey, including facilitating King Bimbisāra in attaining Sotāpattiphala.

            • 13. Can we share demerits to the departed one? This question was also asked by King Milinda.         • During the night, King Pasenadi hears haunting sounds from hell and seeks advice from his minister. The minister, fearing his own ignorance, declares the danger to the king's life and prescribes a grand sacrifice to avert it. The king, valuing his life above all else, orders the sacrifice of a hundred of various beings, including humans. The preparations exceed expectations, reminiscent of a past sacrifice for the king. The story highlights the king's desperation to protect himself, the minister's attempt to provide a solution, and the extreme measures taken to ward off perceived threats from the supernatural realm.
        • The Buddha recites verses condemning violent sacrifices and praising non-violent ones. Queen Mallikā questions King Pasenadi's troubled state, criticizing his reliance on a foolish brahmin for advice that brings suffering. She urges him to seek the Buddha's counsel. Accompanied by the queen, the king visits the Buddha in distress, unable to speak. The queen narrates their ordeal to the Buddha, seeking guidance. The story highlights the folly of violent sacrifices, the queen's wisdom in advising the king, and the king's realization of seeking the Buddha's counsel for clarity and resolution.
        • The Buddha reassures King Pasenadi of no danger. He recounts the story of four wealthy men from Sāvatthi who indulged in adultery, leading to rebirth in hell. Reborn in Avīci and Lohakumbhi hells, they suffered for millennia. Attempting to speak a verse in agony, they could only utter 'sa.' The Buddha completes their verse, expressing endless suffering and shared wrongdoing. Despite ascending and descending in torment, they find no respite. The Buddha's verses convey the eternal nature of suffering and wrongdoing, emphasizing the enduring consequences of immoral actions.
        • After the Buddha recites verses, detailing the consequences of past actions, the man carrying red clay and red lotus attains enlightenment, while King Pasenadi renounces coveting other men's wives. Reflecting on the suffering of those in hell due to adultery, the king resolves to change his ways. He confesses to the Buddha and acknowledges the folly of his past desires. A poor man present shares a wise observation on the concept of time, prompting the Buddha to emphasize the enduring cycle of suffering for those ignorant of true teachings. The narrative underscores the impact of past actions and the importance of wisdom in breaking free from suffering.
