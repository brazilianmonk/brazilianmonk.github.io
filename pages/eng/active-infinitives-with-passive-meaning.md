In some cases, some adjectives such as "tough" are followd by an **active infinitive**, but the subject has a **passive meaning** in relation to the infinitive. These constructions became known as "Tough Constructions" or "Active Infinitives with Passive Meaning".

## 1. Basic pattern

> **Subject + be + adjective + to-infinitive**

Examples:

- This book is **easy to read**.
- The problem is **difficult to solve**.
- The instructions are **hard to follow**.
- This machine is **simple to use**.
- The question is **impossible to answer**.

The infinitives are active in form:

> to read, to solve, to follow, to use, to answer

But the subjects are understood as the **objects** of those actions:

> This book is easy to read.  
> → It is easy to read **this book**.

> The problem is difficult to solve.  
> → It is difficult to solve **the problem**.

> The instructions are hard to follow.  
> → It is hard to follow **the instructions**.

---

## 2. The "hidden object"

A useful way to understand the construction is to imagine the subject in its normal position after the infinitive.

> The book is easy to read **___**.

The blank represents **the book**:

> It is easy to read **the book**.

More examples:

> The car is difficult to drive **___**.  
> → It is difficult to drive **the car**.

> The door is easy to open **___**.  
> → It is easy to open **the door**.

> The language is difficult to learn **___**.  
> → It is difficult to learn **the language**.

> The software is easy to use **___**.  
> → It is easy to use **the software**.

---

## 3. Active form, passive meaning

Compare:

> The book is easy **to read**.

The infinitive is grammatically active: **to read**.

But the book is not doing the reading. Someone reads the book.

So the meaning is approximately:

> The book is easy **to be read**.

However, English normally uses the active infinitive:

> **The book is easy to read.** ✓

rather than:

> **The book is easy to be read.** ✗ / unnatural

Similarly:

- The problem is difficult **to solve**.  
  → The problem is difficult **to be solved**.

- The instructions are hard **to follow**.  
  → The instructions are hard **to be followed**.

- The machine is simple **to operate**.  
  → The machine is simple **to be operated**.

The first versions are the normal English constructions.

## 4. Common adjectives
### easy

- This book is **easy to understand**.
- The question is **easy to answer**.
- The machine is **easy to use**.
- The door is **easy to open**.

### difficult

- The book is **difficult to understand**.
- The problem is **difficult to solve**.
- The language is **difficult to learn**.
- The machine is **difficult to operate**.

### hard

- The instructions are **hard to follow**.
- This material is **hard to find**.
- He is **hard to please**.
- The problem is **hard to explain**.

### impossible

- The question is **impossible to answer**.
- The problem is **impossible to solve**.
- The book is **impossible to find**.

### simple

- The system is **simple to use**.
- The procedure is **simple to follow**.
- The machine is **simple to operate**.

Other adjectives can also occur:

- The water is **safe to drink**.
- This animal is **dangerous to approach**.
- She is **pleasant to talk to**.
- He is **easy to work with**.
- The village is **difficult to get to**.

---

## 5. People as subjects

Tough constructions can also describe people.

> **She is easy to talk to.**

= It is easy to talk to **her**.

> **He is difficult to work with.**

= It is difficult to work with **him**.

> **John is easy to please.**

= It is easy for people to please **John**.

> **John is difficult to understand.**

= It is difficult for people to understand **John**.

### Important contrast

> **John is eager to please.**

John wants to please other people.

> **John is easy to please.**

Other people can easily please John.

The first has an **active relationship** between the subject and infinitive; the second has a **passive relationship**.

---

## 6. With "for + person"

We can specify who performs the action:

> The book is easy **for students to understand**.

> The problem is difficult **for beginners to solve**.

> The instructions are easy **for children to follow**.

Compare:

> The book is easy to understand.

> The book is easy **for students to understand**.

The second sentence simply makes the person performing the action explicit.

---

## 7. Prepositions at the end

If the verb normally requires a preposition, the preposition remains:

> She is easy **to talk to**.

> He is difficult **to work with**.

> This is something that is hard **to deal with**.

> The village is difficult **to get to**.

> The subject is interesting **to talk about**.

The underlying structure is:

> It is easy to talk **to her**.

> It is difficult to work **with him**.

> It is difficult to get **to the village**.

---

## 8. "It is..." vs. a tough construction

These two structures are closely related but grammatically different.

### It + adjective + infinitive

> **It is difficult to understand this book.**

The infinitive has its normal active meaning:

> Someone understands the book.

### Subject + adjective + infinitive

> **This book is difficult to understand.**

The subject corresponds to the object of the infinitive:

> Someone understands **this book**.

The second structure is the classic **tough construction**.

Compare:

> It is easy to learn English.

> English is easy to learn.

> It is difficult to solve the problem.

> The problem is difficult to solve.

---

## 9. Not every adjective + infinitive is a tough construction

An adjective followed by an infinitive does **not** automatically have passive meaning.

Compare:

> **John is eager to help.**

John helps.  
→ John is the **doer**.

> **John is easy to help.**

Someone helps John.  
→ John is the **receiver/object**.

Another example:

> **She is happy to see him.**

She sees him.

> **She is easy to see.**

Someone sees her.

### A useful test

Ask:

> Is the subject doing the infinitive action?

If yes → active relationship.

> John is eager **to help**.  
> → John helps.

If no, ask:

> Is the subject the object/receiver of the infinitive action?

If yes → tough construction.

> John is easy **to help**.  
> → Someone helps John.

---

## 10. "Too + adjective + to"

Tough constructions can also occur with:

> **too + adjective + to-infinitive**

Examples:

> The coffee is **too hot to drink**.

= The coffee is too hot for someone to drink.

> The box is **too heavy to carry**.

= The box is too heavy for someone to carry.

> The water is **too dirty to drink**.

= The water is too dirty for someone to drink.

> The problem is **too complicated to explain briefly**.

= The problem is too complicated for someone to explain briefly.

---

## 11. "Adjective + enough + to"

A similar pattern occurs with **enough**:

> The box is **light enough to carry**.

> The water is **clean enough to drink**.

> The instructions are **clear enough to follow**.

> The road is **wide enough to drive on**.

Again, the subject is understood as the object of the infinitive.

---

## 12. Related structures with passive meaning

Tough constructions are part of a broader group of English structures where an active-looking form has a passive interpretation.

### Worth + -ing

> This book is **worth reading**.

= This book is worth **being read**.

> The movie is **worth watching**.

> The museum is **worth visiting**.

> The problem is **worth considering**.

### Need + -ing

> The car **needs washing**.

= The car needs **to be washed**.

> The house **needs painting**.

= The house needs **to be painted**.

> The windows **need cleaning**.

= The windows need **to be cleaned**.

These are related to the same general idea, but they are **not tough constructions** grammatically.

---

## 13. "Ready to..." can be different

Be careful with sentences such as:

> **The food is ready to eat.**

This can mean:

> The food is ready **to be eaten**.

But:

> **John is ready to eat.**

means:

> John is ready **to eat something**.

So the same adjective + infinitive pattern does not always have the same semantic relationship.

---

# Summary

### The main pattern

> **Subject + be + adjective + to-infinitive**

### Passive semantic relationship

> The book is easy **to read**.  
> → Someone reads **the book**.

> The problem is difficult **to solve**.  
> → Someone solves **the problem**.

> She is easy **to talk to**.  
> → Someone talks **to her**.

### Active semantic relationship

> John is eager **to help**.  
> → John helps.

> She is happy **to see him**.  
> → She sees him.

### The key idea

In a **tough construction**, the infinitive is **active in form**, but the subject of the main clause is understood as the **object/patient** of the infinitive.

> **This book is easy to understand.**

This is normally preferred to:

> **This book is easy to be understood.**
