    • definition ‒ Pāḷi #edited #a_dp_3y2s
      • saṅkhepato paṭisaṅkhā yoniso paccayaparibhogalakkhaṇaṃ paccayasannissitasīlaṃ veditabbaṃ (in brief, the paccaya sannissita sīla is the [acceptance and] usage of requisites having contemplated wisely [avoiding papañca: taṇhā, mada, diṭṭhi in oneself or others])
    • etymology (not in exam) #edited
      • paccaya [pati +√i > aya]: beings depend upon them, so they are called paccaya
      • paccaya sannissita sīla: the sīla which depends upon these paccayas – “Vacanattho panettha – cīvarādayo hi yasmā te paṭicca nissāya paribhuñjamānā pāṇino ayanti pavattanti, tasmā paccayāti vuccanti. Te paccaye sannissitanti paccayasannissitaṃ.”
    • paccavekkhaṇa ‒ definition #edited
      • knowing wisely according to the correct means
      • having repeatedly understood the benefits of the requisites
      • sabbāsavasutta: Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu paṭisaṅkhā yoniso cīvaraṃ paṭisevati...
      • paṭisaṅkhā yoniso ⇒ yoniso paṭisaṅkhāya
        • yoniso: according to the proper means or method, properly
        • paṭisaṅkhāya: having known with wisdom
      • opinions
        • some teachers of the past thought that the paccaya sannissita sīla becomes purified not only by contemplating the benefits of the requisites, but also contemplating them in terms of non-self elements (dhātu vasena) and repulsiveness (paṭikkūla vasena). Some teachers, who do not appreciate this idea, were of the opinion that if the benefits are not contemplated, the paccaya sannissita will be broken. Another group of teachers considered that just by mere contemplation of benefits of the requisites, the paccaya sannissita sīla can be fulfilled.

      • piṇḍapāta paccavekkhaṇa
        • Following is how the Buddha explained the contemplation of food in the Sabbāsava Sutta, while explaining the topic “Atthi, bhikkhave, āsavā paṭisevanā pahātabbā.” Paṭisaṅkhā yoniso piṇḍapātaṃ paṭisevati – “Neva davāya..."
        • In the above paragraph, “piṇḍapāta” means any food that is consumed. Food is called piṇḍapāta as it has fallen in the patta (bowl) due to going for alms (piṇḍolya). The verb “paṭisevati” refers to the consumption of food. Neva davāya, na madāya, na maṇḍanāya, na vibhūsanāya – food is consumed not for fun as village children do. It is consumed not to increase the purisa mada as wrestlers do [purisa mada is the conceit that arises as “I am a man”. According to some teachers, purisa mada is the conceit due to one's ability to have sex or lust due to the ability to have sex.].xii Food is consumed not to grow (enlarge) body parts as kings’ concubines and prostitutes do. Food is consumed not to have fair complexion like dancers and actors do.
        • With the phrase, “neva davāya” the Buddha expressed the removal of the support to ignorance (mohassa upanissaya). With the phrase, “na madāya” the Buddha expressed the removal of the support to anger (dosassa upanissaya). With the phrases, “na maṇḍanāya” and “na vibhūsanāya” the Buddha expressed the removal of the support to lust (rāgassa upanissaya). In addition, the phrase “neva davāya” and “na madāya” the Buddha highlighted the prevention of the arising of one’s own fetters. The two phrases “na maṇḍanāya” and “na vibhūsanāya” highlight the prevention of the arising of others’ fetters. With all the four phrases, the Buddha indicated the abandoning of the “kāmasukhallikhānu yoga” (indulge in sensual pleasures) due to unwise practice (ayoniso paṭipatti).
        • The term “yāvadeva” limits the benefits of the food. There are eight benefits of food as follows. Those are the only benefits of consuming food for a monk. The term “yāvadeva” limits the benefits of using food to eight.
        • Food is consumed:
          • (1). Imassa kāyassa ṭhitiyā yāpanāya – for the existence of the jīvitindriya, which is called ṭhiti, of the body made out of four great elements.
          • (2). Vihiṃsūparatiyā – to quench the hunger as if applying medicine to heal a wound or associating cold or heat when the body is oppressed by excessive heat or cold respectively.
          • (3). Brahmacariyānuggahāya – as a support for the 2 brahmacariya
            • sakala sāsana brahmacariyā: admonishments that encompass the threefold training that must be frequently practiced (exc. magga)
            • magga brahmacariyā: the noble path
            • A monk, who is eating food thinking “I shall obtain physical strength by consuming this and fulfill the threefold training and cross over the desert of existences,” is consuming food as an assist to the brahmacariyā.
          • (4). Iti purāṇañca vedanaṃ paṭihaṅkhāmi – thinking “I shall dispel old pains that has occurred due to hunger.”
          • (5). Navañca vedanaṃ na uppādessāmi – thinking “I shall not arouse new pains by overeating.” By expressing the proper consumption as mentioned above the Buddha elaborated the abandoning of the “attakilamathānuyoga” (self mortification) and notabandoning the comfort obtained righteously (dhammika sukha).
          • dhammika sukha↔comfort obtained righteously
          • (6). Yātrā me bhavissati – thinking “Due to this consumption I shall make a (long) journey.” Living long is similar to making a long journey (yātrā). The monk will be able to successfully accomplish that long journey of life by eating food moderately. By consuming food moderately one can avoid the dangers which cut off the life faculty (jīvitindriya upacchedaka) and which will break the postures (iriyāpatha bhañjanaka). (what are these dangers?) #q🙋🏼‍♂
          • (7). Anavajjatā – by consuming food faultlessly that has been obtained righteously and accepted properly, the monk will have his faultlessness in his character; the monk will have a faultless character.
          • (8). Phasuvihāro – by consuming food moderately, the monk will have the comfort. By these last three words, the Buddha elaborated the benefits of consuming food and indicated the middle path (majjhimā paṭipadā).xv (why the majjhima patipada?) #q🙋🏼‍♂

      • ādittapariyāyasutta #editing
        • The Buddha taught that it would be preferable to rub one’s eye with a red-hot iron rod than to attend to the nimitta and anubyañjana of visible forms. Likewise, it would be better to pierce both ears with a blazing rod than to seize the signs of a sound; to cut the nose with a fiery blade than to seize the signs of an odour; to sever the tongue at its root than to seize the signs of a flavour; and to uproot bodily sensitivity with a sharp weapon than to seize the signs of a tangible object. Why? Should a being die while delighting in such signs, rebirth in hell or the animal realm is possible.5
      • Relationship between Indriya Saṃvara and Pātimokkha Saṃvara Sīla
        • If the indriya saṃvara sīla is undeveloped, the pātimokkha saṃvara sīla cannot endure – just as a crop not surrounded well by leaves and branches gets destroyed quickly, or a village with open gates is overrun by bandits. Rūpesu saddesu atho rasesu Gandhesu phassesu ca rakkha indriyaṃ Ete hi dvārā vivaṭā arakkhitā Hananti gāmaṃ va parassa hārino.6 The Buddha conveys the same ;idea in the Dhammapada: Yathā agāraṃ ducchannaṃ – vuṭṭhī samativijjhati Evaṃ abhāvitaṃ ;
        • cittaṃ – rāgo samativijjhati.7
        • Conversely, when the faculties are well guarded, pātimokkha restraint endures – just as a well-guarded crop thrives and a well-closed village is not attacked by raiders.
      • Coping with Sudden Lust
        • What has been described above illustrates how indriya saṃvara sīla can be developed to an excellent degree. However, the mind changes very rapidly – “Lahuparivattaṃ cittaṃ.” Therefore, lust may suddenly arise toward objects such as visible forms and so forth. When such lust arises, one must suppress it and reestablish the indriya saṃvara sīla. Early in his ordination, Venerable Vaṅgīsa saw a woman while on alms-round and felt his mind inflamed with lust. He approached Venerable Ānanda for guidance: Kāmarāgena ḍayhāmi, cittaṃ me pariḍayhati; Sādhu nibbāpanaṃ brūhi, anukampāya Gotama.
        • Venerable Ānanda replied: “Your mind burns with kāmarāga due to a saññā-vipallāsa (distortion of perception). Abandon the subha-nimitta (sign of beauty) that nourishes lust, without giving it any further contemplation. Direct your mind instead toward a repulsive object, whether living or dead. Eliminate mental distraction (vikkhepa) and cultivate a mind that is concentrated and tranquil, firmly established upon the perception of the repulsive. View all conditioned
        • phenomena (saṅkhāra) as not belonging to you, as sources of suffering, and as non-self. Having done so, extinguish the great fire of lust. Do not allow yourself to be consumed by it again and again.” Saññāya vipariyesā - cittaṃ te pariḍayhati Nimittaṃ parivajjehi - subhaṃ rāgūpasañhitaṃ Asubhāya cittaṃ bhāvehi - ekaggaṃ susamāhitaṃ. Saṅkhāre parato passa - dukkhato no ca attato Nibbāpehi mahārāgaṃ - mā ḍayhittho punappunaṃ. Following this counsel, Vaṅgīsa subdued the passion and continued his alms round unhindered.8
      • Venerable Cittagutta’s Perfect Indriya Saṃvara
        • Venerable Cittagutta resided in the Karaṇḍaka Cave for more than sixty years. The cave contained beautiful paintings depicting the renunciations of the seven Buddhas. On one occasion, a large group of monks was touring various monasteries. When they arrived at the Karaṇḍaka Cave and saw the paintings, they said to the Thera, “Venerable Sir, these drawings are very attractive.” The Thera replied, “Friends, I have been living in this cave for over sixty years, but I did not know there were any drawings here. Today I learned about them thanks to those who have eyes.” Indeed, for the entire sixty years he had dwelled there, Venerable Cittagutta had not once looked at the paintings. At the entrance of the cave stood a nāga tree. The Thera never looked at its branches. Once a year, when the hairy structures of the flowers fell to the ground, he would come to know that the tree had bloomed.

Learning of this, and out of compassion for the young children, the Thera journeyed to Mahāgāma. As the Elder approached, the king sent attendants to escort him into the inner city.       • The paccaya sannissita sīla must be developed with wisdom (paññā): “Paccayasannissitasīlaṃ paññāya sampādetabbaṃ. Paññāsādhanaṃ hi taṃ.” This is because the advantages and disadvantages associated with requisites can only be properly understood through wisdom. A monk fulfilling the paccaya sannissita sīla must use the requisites he obtains – without violating the ājīvapārisuddhi sīla – with wise reflection, as explained earlier.14 There are four types of use (paribhoga) of requisites:
        • (1). Theyya-paribhoga
        • (2). Iṇa-paribhoga
        • (3). Dāyajja-paribhoga
        • (4). Sāmi-paribhoga
      • We shall now examine each of these in detail.
      • Theyya Paribhoga
        • This refers to the use of the four requisites by a monk who has committed any of the pārājikā offences. By committing a pārājikā, a monk loses his status as a bhikkhu and becomes unfit to use the four requisites offered by devotees. The Buddha allowed these requisites only for virtuous monks. Likewise, lay devotees offer the requisites to monks who are virtuous – not to those who are unvirtuous. For these two reasons, the use of the four requisites by one who has committed a pārājikā offence is regarded as equivalent to using the requisites in theft: the Buddha has not permitted him to use them, and the donors did not intend them for someone who is unvirtuous.15 This form of usage is extremely dangerous. It is better to swallow a hot iron ball than to consume the requisites offered by pious devotees while being unvirtuous and pretending to be virtuous:
        • Seyyo ayoguḷo bhutto – tatto aggisikhūpamo, Yañce bhuñjeyya dussīlo – raṭṭhapiṇḍamasaññato.16 The Tathāgata also described the suffering incurred by a monk who, being unvirtuous, uses the requisites offered by faithful devotees or accepts their veneration. This was taught in the Aggikkhandhopama Sutta. However, if a monk who has committed a pārājikā offence becomes a sāmaṇera, he becomes eligible to receive and use the four requisites offered by devotees.

Here, purification means making the offering more fruitful through its proper and reflective use. Thus, such consumption is likened to using something that has been borrowed, not truly possessed.

    • 21. Can you guide a young monk to be free from inaparibhoga and dukkaṭa related to each catu-paccaya? Write a essay.
      • For a young monk embarking on the monastic path, understanding the proper use of requisites is paramount to spiritual progress. The Buddha emphasized that the mere physical consumption of requisites is insufficient; it must be accompanied by wise contemplation (paññā) to avoid falling into states of "debt" or incurring minor offenses. This guidance is rooted in the paccaya sannissita sīla, the virtue of relying on requisites wisely.
        • This essay will guide a young monk on how to be free from iṇaparibhoga and dukkaṭa offenses related to the four requisites (robes, food, lodging, and medicine) by detailing the principles of wise contemplation and specific applications for each requisite.
        • The Core Principle: Wise Contemplation and Avoiding Iṇa-paribhoga
        • The paccaya sannissita sīla must be developed with wisdom (paññā) because only through wisdom can the advantages and disadvantages associated with requisites be properly understood. A monk fulfilling this sīla must use requisites, obtained without violating the ājīvapārisuddhi sīla, with wise reflection (paṭisaṅkhā yoniso).
        • Iṇa-paribhoga (Usage as Debt) specifically refers to the consumption of the four requisites by a virtuous puthujjana (ordinary) monk without wise contemplation. When contemplation is absent, the monk fails to "purify" (visodhana) the offering made by the donor, meaning they do not make the offering more fruitful through its proper and reflective use. Such consumption is likened to using something that has been borrowed, not truly possessed, implying an "indebted" state to the donor and the purpose of the offering. The danger is clear: a monk using requisites in this way "does not gain the opportunity to become free from the world," just as a debtor cannot travel where they wish. The opposite, and the goal for a contemplating monk, is "āṇaṇya paribhoga" ("debt-free usage").
        • The Schedule of Contemplation to Avoid Iṇa-paribhoga
        • To avoid iṇa-paribhoga, a monk must understand the crucial timings for contemplation:
          • Two Primary Occasions: Paccaya sannissita sīla must be fulfilled on two main occasions:
            • While obtaining the requisites (paccaya paṭilābha kāle).
            • While consuming the requisites (paccaya paribhoga kāle).
          • Contemplation Flexibility: While it's ideal to contemplate each time a requisite is used (e.g., each mouthful of food), the sources provide a crucial allowance for when this is missed. If, for any reason, a monk fails to perform the immediate contemplation, they must perform the contemplation at least once during one of the following sessions within that day: purebhatta (before the meal), pacchā bhatta (after the meal), pubba yāma (first watch of the night), majjhima yāma (middle watch of the night), or pacchima yāma (last watch of the night).
          • The Critical Rule: If contemplation was not done at least once within that day, the consumption of the monk's requisites on that day falls into iṇa-paribhoga. This highlights the absolute necessity of daily, wise reflection.
        • Avoiding Iṇa-paribhoga and Specific Dukkaṭa for Each Requisite
        • Here's how wise contemplation applies to each of the four requisites:
          • Robes (Cīvara)
            • Contemplation while Obtaining: When receiving a robe, a monk should contemplate it either as non-self elements (dhātu vasena) (e.g., "this robe is merely elements, and the user is also merely elements, without a self") or in terms of repulsiveness (paṭikkūla vasena) (e.g., "these robes, though not repulsive on their own, become extremely repulsive upon touching this decaying body"). This purifies the beginning of the paccaya sannissita sīla.
            • Contemplation while Consuming (Wearing): Contemplation must be done each time the robe is worn after being taken off the body.
            • Purpose of Robes: Robes are used only for specific benefits:
              • To ward off coldness (sītassa paṭighātāya).
              • To ward off heat (uṇhassa paṭighātāya).
              • To ward off contact with flies (ḍaṃsa), mosquitoes (makasa), wind, sun, and crawling beings (siriṃsapa) (ḍaṃsamakasavātātapasariṃsapa-samphassānaṃ paṭighātāya).
              • To cover private parts (hirikopīnappaṭicchādanatthaṃ).
            • Related Dukkaṭa: The sources provided do not explicitly mention a dukkaṭa offense for robes directly related to the lack of contemplation within paccaya sannissita sīla. However, if paccaya sannissita sīla is broken (i.e., contemplation is not done at all for the day), then the consumption falls into iṇa-paribhoga, which is a grave spiritual fault.
          • Food (Piṇḍapāta)
            • Contemplation while Obtaining: Food should be contemplated in terms of the perception of its repulsiveness (āhāre paṭikkūla saññā) upon receiving it.
            • Contemplation while Consuming: Contemplation must be done while consuming each mouthful.
            • Purpose of Food: Food is consumed not for:
              • Fun (neva davāya).
              • Conceit (na madāya) (e.g., "I am a man," or lust due to sexual ability).
              • Embellishment (na maṇḍanāya) (e.g., to grow body parts).
              • Beautification (na vibhūsanāya) (e.g., to have fair complexion). Instead, food is consumed only for:
              • The existence and sustenance of the body (imassa kāyassa ṭhitiyā yāpanāya).
              • To quench hunger (vihiṃsūparatiyā).
              • As a support for the holy life (brahmacariyānuggahāya) (both sāsana brahmacariya and magga brahmacariya).
              • To dispel old pains (purāṇañca vedanaṃ paṭihaṅkhāmi) (due to hunger).
              • To not arouse new pains (navañca vedanaṃ na uppādessāmi) (by overeating).
              • To enable a long journey (yātrā me bhavissati) (living long moderately).
              • For faultlessness (anavajjatā) in character.
              • For comfort (phāsuvihāro).

          • Lodging (Senāsana)
            • Contemplation while Obtaining: Similar to robes, lodging should be contemplated either as non-self elements (dhātu) or in terms of repulsiveness (paṭikkūla) when receiving it.
            • Contemplation while Consuming (Using): Contemplation must be done each time the monk enters the lodging.
            • Purpose of Lodging: Lodging (which includes buildings like vihāra, aḍḍhayoga, pāsāda, hammiya, guhā, and furniture like chairs and beds) is used only for:
              • To ward off coldness (sītassa paṭighātāya).
              • To ward off heat (uṇhassa paṭighātāya).
              • To ward off contact with flies, mosquitoes, wind, sun, and crawling beings (ḍaṃsamakasavātātapasiriṃsapa-samphassānaṃ paṭighātāya).
              • To ward off weather conditions that cause sickness and distract the mind (utuparissaya vinodanaṃ).
              • To live in seclusion (paṭisallānārāmatthaṃ).

            • Contemplation while Obtaining: Medicine must be contemplated accordingly when received (implying dhātu or paṭikkūla contemplation, similar to robes and food).
            • Contemplation while Consuming: There will be a dukkaṭa offense if a monk does not contemplate while consuming the medicine, even if they had contemplated while accepting it. This is a direct and specific dukkaṭa related to the paccaya sannissita sīla itself.

    • 21. Can you guide a young monk to be free from iṇa paribhoga and dukkaṭa related to each catu-paccaya? Write an essay.
      • Essay: Guiding a Young Monk to Be Free from Iṇa Paribhoga and Dukkaṭa Related to the Four Requisites
        • To guide a young monk in maintaining purity in the use of the four requisites (catu-paccaya: robes, food, lodging, and medicine) and avoiding iṇa paribhoga (indebted usage) and dukkaṭa (minor offences), one must emphasize the practice of paccaya sannissita sīla, which involves wise contemplation (paṭisaṅkhā yoniso) at the moments of receiving and consuming requisites. Iṇa paribhoga occurs when a virtuous puthujjana monk uses requisites without wise reflection, failing to purify the donor’s offering, thus remaining “indebted” and unable to escape saṃsāra (Vsm-ṭ. I. 74). Dukkaṭa offences arise, particularly with medicine, when contemplation is neglected during consumption (Vsm. I. 41). Below is a concise guide to ensure freedom from these faults for each requisite, rooted in the provided teachings.
        • 1. Robes (Cīvara)
          • To avoid iṇa paribhoga, a young monk must contemplate robes at two times: upon receiving (paccaya paṭilābha kāle) and using (paccaya paribhoga kāle). Upon receiving, reflect as non-self elements: “Yathāpaccayaṃ pavattamānaṃ dhātumattamevetaṃ, yadidaṃ cīvaraṃ” (Vsm. I. 41), or as repulsive: “Sabbāni pana imāni cīvarāni ajigucchanīyāni, imaṃ pūtikāyaṃ patvā ativiya jigucchanīyāni jāyanti” (Vsm-ṭ. I. 71). When wearing, recite: “Paṭisaṅkhā yoniso cīvaraṃ paṭisevati – Yāvadeva sītassa paṭighātāya, uṇhassa paṭighātāya, ḍaṃsamakasavātātapasariṃsapasamphassānaṃ paṭighātāya, yāvadeva hirikopīnappaṭicchādanatthaṃ” (M. I. 12), focusing on the four benefits: warding off cold, heat, insect bites, and covering private parts. This ensures the monk avoids craving or wrong view, purifying the donor’s intent. No dukkaṭa is specified for robes, but consistent contemplation prevents iṇa paribhoga, aligning usage with dāyajja or sāmi paribhoga (Vsm. I. 42).
        • 2. Food (Piṇḍapāta)
          • For food, contemplate upon receiving in terms of repulsiveness (āhāre paṭikkūla saññā) to avoid attachment. During consumption, reflect with each mouthful: “Paṭisaṅkhā yoniso piṇḍapātaṃ paṭisevati – Neva davāya, na madāya, na maṇḍanāya, na vibhūsanāya, yāvadeva imassa kāyassa ṭhitiyā yāpanāya, vihiṃsūparatiyā, brahmacariyānuggahāya…” (M. I. 12-3), focusing on eight benefits, such as sustaining the body and supporting spiritual practice. If contemplation is missed, reflect at least once during purebhatta, pacchābhatta, pubba yāma, majjhima yāma, or pacchima yāma to avoid iṇa paribhoga (Vsm. I. 41). The Vissāsabhojana Jātaka (J-a. V. 411-2) warns that even food from relatives requires reflection, as unreflected use is like consuming poison, risking rebirth as a yakkha or peta. No dukkaṭa applies to food, but diligent contemplation ensures faultless use.
        • 3. Lodging (Senāsana)
          • Upon receiving lodging, contemplate as non-self or repulsive to purify initial acceptance. When entering or using, reflect: “Paṭisaṅkhā yoniso senāsanaṃ paṭisevati – Yāvadeva sītassa paṭighātāya, uṇhassa paṭighātāya, ḍaṃsamakasavātātapasariṃsapasamphassānaṃ paṭighātāya, yāvadeva utuparissayavinodanapaṭisallānārāmatthaṃ” (M. I. 13), focusing on five benefits, including seclusion. If missed, contemplate during one of the five daily sessions to avoid iṇa paribhoga. No dukkaṭa is mentioned for lodging, but reflection prevents “indebted” use, aligning with the donor’s intent (Vsm. I. 42).
        • 4. Medicine (Gilānapaccaya Bhesajja)
          • For medicine, contemplate upon receiving as non-self or repulsive. When consuming, reflect: “Paṭisaṅkhā yoniso gilānappaccayabhesajjaparikkhāraṃ paṭisevati – Yāvadeva uppannānaṃ veyyābādhikānaṃ vedanānaṃ paṭighātāya, abyābajjhaparamatāya” (M. I. 13), focusing on two benefits: expelling pain and attaining a pain-free state. Failure to contemplate during consumption incurs a dukkaṭa offence, even if reflected upon receiving (Vsm. I. 41). If missed, contemplate during one of the five sessions to avoid both dukkaṭa and iṇa paribhoga. The Cūḷa Accharā Saṅghāta Sutta (A-a. I. 53) suggests cultivating mettā briefly to purify usage, making the monk worthy of offerings.
        • Additional Practices
          • To further ensure purity, cultivate mettā daily, even for a finger snap, as it compensates for minor sīla deficiencies and aligns usage with donor intent (A-ṭ. I. 109). Engage in buddhānussati, dhammānussati, or saṅghānussati during vandanā to enhance worthiness (Śāsanāvataraṇaya, 137-9). Avoid nimitta, obhāsa, parikatā, and viññatti for robes and food, and for lodging if practicing dhutaṅga, to maintain ājīvapārisuddhi (Vsm. I. 38). Emulate Venerable Sāriputta’s example, rejecting requisites obtained improperly, prioritizing virtue over comfort (Vsm. I. 38-40).
