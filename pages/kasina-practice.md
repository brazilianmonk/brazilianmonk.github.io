## Prerequisites for Success

To achieve meaningful progress and success in this and any meditation practice, specific foundational requirements must be met to prevent gross obstructive mental states and allow the mind to develop subtler, wholesome qualities:
* **The Five Precepts:** The practitioner must follow at least the five precepts (abstaining from: killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, and consuming intoxicants). This is essential because those actions prevent the mind from developing more stable and subtler mental states.
* **Kammassakatañāṇa (Understanding the Ownership of Deeds):** The practitioner must possess the correct understanding that their intentional actions produce results in the future according to their inherent nature (unwholesome deeds produce negative, while wholesome positive results). This understanding forms the basic right view necessary to support deep meditation and without it, the mind is incapable of achieving meaningful progress in meditation. Mere confidence in this principle, without deep understanding, is enough at this point.

## The Object

The physical setup of the external disk, known as the *parikamma nimitta*, serves as the initial basis for this development.
* **Dimensions and Material:** The disk should ideally be white and about 30 cm in diameter (roughly equivalent to one span and four fingers) [cite: 7, 9]. It can be made digitally on a sufficiently large screen or TV (which is advantageous as it is free from physical imperfections), or physically using paper, cloth, or any other clean white material [cite: 8, 9].
* **Sizing Adjustments:** While a 30 cm disk is standard, making it larger tends to be better [cite: 11]. If the disk is smaller, it can still be used as an object but must be positioned closer to the meditator [cite: 12, 14].
* **Environment and Distance:** Place the disk in your regular meditation area against a neutral background that is completely free from visual distractions [cite: 10]. For a standard 30 cm disk, sit at a distance of approximately 1.0 to 1.25 meters [cite: 7, 13].
* **Angle:** Position the disk at an angle where you can look at it comfortably without straining your posture [cite: 7].

## The Practice Method and Visual Evolution
The core technique relies on transitioning from physical sight to mental recollection.

### Initial Practice Technique
* **Alternating Focus:** The instruction is to alternate between looking at the external disk with eyes open and closed [cite: 16, 107].
* **Applied Thought (Vitakka):** Periodically apply thought to the object by mentally repeating "odāta, odāta..." or "white, white..." [cite: 16, 18, 73]. This repetition is vital as it centers the mind and clarifies the precise concept to which you are directing your awareness [cite: 19, 74]. It does not need to be repeated excessively fast or constantly; doing it from time to time is sufficient [cite: 27, 108].
* **Object of Mind:** Understand that even without a clear mental image, simply thinking of the concept of "white" while remaining mindful constitutes being on the object [cite: 37, 106].

### The Acquired Sign (Uggaha Nimitta)
* **Definition:** When you can see the disk in your mind with closed eyes as clearly as if your eyes were open, you have successfully arisen the *uggaha nimitta* [cite: 15, 17, 23, 36, 125].
* **Moving Beyond the Physical Disk:** Once you can regularly see or remember the disk with eyes closed, you must stop looking at the external object and avoid comparing them [cite: 20, 67, 69, 112]. Stick strictly to the disk in your mind [cite: 70].
* **The Nature of Uggaha:** The *uggaha nimitta* is inherently unstable and will not last long [cite: 21, 52, 58, 64]. It acts like a literal mental photograph; therefore, if the external disk has a stain or includes a specific background (like a screen frame or wall), these elements will appear in the *uggaha* [cite: 126, 127]. It cannot be voluntarily expanded or contracted [cite: 130].
* **Managing Disappearance:** When the mental image fades and the mind goes "black," do not immediately open your eyes to look at the external disk [cite: 71, 79]. Continue meditating with the closed-eye intention and repeat "white, white"; the image will naturally reappear on its own from time to time [cite: 38, 59, 72, 109]. Only look back at the physical disk if you find yourself completely unable to recollect the concept or object at all [cite: 24, 34, 62].
* **Visual Anomalies to Ignore:**
  * **Black Circles:** You may see a temporary black circle immediately upon closing your eyes. This is purely a physiological visual after-image effect and holds no spiritual meaning; ignore it [cite: 22, 35].
  * **Shiny Dust/Particles:** If the image disappears and you force your eyes to focus, you might notice shiny, sparkling dust particles. Stop this immediately. This shiny dust is not your meditation subject and will not lead to progress [cite: 80, 81, 82].

### The Counterpart Sign (Paṭibhāga Nimitta)
* **Definition:** The *paṭibhāganimitta* is the next major landmark in concentration [cite: 23]. It is a powerful, pristine mental image produced purely by the concentrated mind [cite: 36].
* **Characteristics:** Unlike the *uggaha*, it is entirely free from backgrounds, frames, or imperfections [cite: 128]. It appears as a distinct, bright light that becomes whiter and more brilliant as concentration intensifies [cite: 129].
* **Stability and Control:** The *paṭibhāga* is highly stable, can be maintained for extended periods with practice, and can be voluntarily expanded by the meditator [cite: 130, 131]. If a white cloud or persistent brightness naturally starts focusing in front of your closed eyes, it may be the onset of the *paṭibhāga* [cite: 120, 121, 133].
* **Stabilizing the Sign:** Even when the *paṭibhāga* is present, if subtle wandering thoughts arise or the sign begins to fluctuate, you can gently think "white" to restabilize both the mind and the nimitta [cite: 101, 102].

## Intensities of Concentration (Samādhi)
The Kasina practice progresses through three distinct intensities of concentration:
1. **Parikamma Samādhi (Preliminary Concentration):** The level of concentration developed from the initial viewing of the external object and during the experience of the *uggaha nimitta*, lasting up until the *paṭibhāga nimitta* is first produced [cite: 105].
2. **Upacāra Samādhi (Neighborhood/Access Concentration):** Attained precisely when the *paṭibhāganimitta* is produced [cite: 103]. Here, the *paṭibhāganimitta* becomes the primary object of concentration prior to entering full absorption [cite: 104, 105].
3. **Appanā Samādhi (Absorption Concentration):** High-level concentration where the mind enters full meditative absorption (*jhāna*) with the *paṭibhāganimitta* as its unshakeable object [cite: 105].

## Balancing Effort, Intention, and Posture
Deepening concentration requires an advanced understanding of effort, shifting from active manipulation to subtle allowance.

### The Dynamics of Effort
* **Right Effort:** The correct amount of effort is simply whatever is required to maintain continuous mindfulness of the object [cite: 26]. If the object is present and stable, no additional effort is needed.
* **Over-effort vs. Relaxation:** If your effort leads to restlessness or mental strain, immediately reduce it and calm the mind [cite: 114, 115]. Conversely, if the mind grows grossly distracted, re-engage and increase your effort [cite: 49].
* **"Doing Without Doing":** Do not try to aggressively grip or hold onto the image, as this causes it to distort, warp, or change color [cite: 75]. Instead, meditate by simply *inclining* or *intending* toward the object [cite: 76, 77, 115]. Once the mind is trained, it will move toward the object automatically [cite: 44]. When tension builds up, "let go of the reins and let the horses run"—trust the momentum of the practice [cite: 45, 46].

### Physical and Postural Adaptations
* **Automatic Adjustment:** As concentration deepens, avoid over-controlling your body [cite: 43]. Let your posture adapt automatically on "auto-pilot" to bear the higher mental weight [cite: 42, 47, 116].
* **Natural Postural Signs:** Under deep concentration, the spine will naturally become erect (or slightly lean forward), shoulders will fall into place, the chin will slightly tuck, the eyes will physically engage and focus forward naturally, and the tongue will engage by pressing upwards against the palate [cite: 48, 83, 122]. Allow these natural adjustments to happen, which will cause residual tension to dissolve [cite: 48]. If the physical tension is too overwhelming, try practicing while lying down [cite: 117].

## Navigating Obstacles, Emotions, and Expectations
* **Fluctuations in Joy (Pīti):** It is completely normal if rapture (*pīti*) is absent at times. Joy and internal experiences will fluctuate heavily throughout the development process [cite: 41].
* **The Illusion of Defilements:** Facing severe difficulties or experiencing an unstable image does not mean your mind is uniquely filled with defilements; even an *arahat* practicing without prior worldly jhanas (*lokiyajhānas*) would experience these identical development steps and instabilities [cite: 32, 33].
* **Anxiety and Self-Consciousness:** Properly executed meditation decreases anxiety [cite: 90]. However, as mindfulness, moral shame, and moral fear are enhanced, you become highly self-conscious and aware of your own faults, past missteps, and subtle distractions [cite: 92]. If wrongly attended to, this newfound sensitivity can feel like a loss of peace or cause temporary worry [cite: 92, 94]. Accept these insights, correct your conduct, and let the mind settle into deeper purity [cite: 95, 97].
* **Consistency over Intensity:** When your daily schedule is busy, maintain short, frequent sessions to keep the mental object fresh and consistent [cite: 31, 60, 66, 134, 135].
* **Alternative Backup Objects:** If you sit down to meditate and find yourself completely unable to recall or stabilize the white kasina image at that time, you can temporarily switch to alternative meditation subjects: *anapanasati* (breath awareness), the four brahmaviharas (*mettā, karuṇā, muditā, upekkhā*), *buddhānussati* (recollection of the Buddha), *maraṇānussati* (recollection of death), or *vipassanā* reflections [cite: 5, 6, 39, 136].
