    • bathing
      • frequency of bathing
        • Yo pana bhikkhu orenaddhamāsaṃ nahāyeyya, aññatra samayā, pācittiyaṃ. Tatthāyaṃ samayo. Diyaḍḍho māso seso gimhānanti vassānassa paṭhamo māso iccete aḍḍhateyyamāsā uṇhasamayo, pariḷāhasamayo, gilānasamayo, kammasamayo, addhānagamanasamayo, vātavuṭṭhisamayo – ayaṃ tattha samayo.
          • where is this rule applicable? and what is the allowance for other regions?
            • only in the middle region (majjhadesa) - “Anujānāmi bhikkhave paccantimesu janapadesu dhuvanahānaṃ.”
          • what are the 6 exceptions?
            • aḍḍhateyyamāsā - 2 1/2 months
              • uṇhasamayo→uṇhasamayo nāma diyaḍḍho māso seso gimhānaṃ - the occasion of heat being the last 1 1/2 months of the hot season
              • pariḷāhasamayo→pariḷāhasamayo nāma vassānassa paṭhamo māso - the occasion of stifling humidity is the 1st month of the rainy season
            • gilānasamayo→gilānasamayo nāma yassa vinā nahānena na phāsu hoti - the occasion of sickness is whenever one is not comfortable without bathing
            • kammasamayo→kammasamayo nāma antamaso pariveṇampi sammaṭṭhaṃ hoti - the occasion of work is when one has worked, even just having swept the monastery grounds
            • addhānagamanasamayo→addhānagamanasamayo nāma “addhayojanaṃ gacchissāmī”ti nahāyitabbaṃ, gacchantena nahāyitabbaṃ, gatena nahāyitabbaṃ. - the occasion of a journey: intending to travel for at least 1/2 a yojana (~10km) one can bathe before, during, and after the journey
            • vātavuṭṭhisamayo→vātavuṭṭhisamayo nāma bhikkhū sarajena vātena okiṇṇā honti, dve vā tīṇi vā udakaphusitāni kāye patitāni honti - the occasion of wind and rain is when one's body becomes dirty with dusty wind and two or three raindrops fall on his body

    • preaching the Dhamma
      • prohibitions and allowances with regard to preaching the dhamma
        • prohibitions
          • na, bhikkhave, āyatakena (drawn out, long, stretched out) gītassarena (singing voice) dhammo gāyitabbo (should be sung). yo gāyeyya, āpatti dukkaṭassa.
            • purpose: because doing so leads to the distortion, the breaking and the loss of words and meanings, as well as the 5 disadvantages - Pañcime, bhikkhave, ādīnavā āyatakena (drawn out) gītassarena (singing voice) dhammaṃ gāyantassa:
              • attanāpi tasmiṃ sare sārajjati (oneself becomes attached to the voice)
              • parepi tasmiṃ sare sārajjanti (others also become attached to the voice)
              • gahapatikāpi ujjhāyanti (householders express their displeasure)
              • sarakuttimpi nikāmayamānassa samādhissa bhaṅgo hoti (the concentration of one who is craving for the making of melodies is disrupted)
              • pacchimā janatā diṭṭhānugatiṃ āpajjati (future generations emulate the same [thinking their preceptors and teachers recited in this manner])
              • mem aid: attanā, pare, gahapatika, samādhi, pacchimā janatā
                  • IIT - Nissayamuttaka Course
            • how much can the letters be lengthened?
              • according to gaṇṭhipada: it can be lengthened to up to 6 mattā but normally it should not exceed 2 mattā
          • kdkpt-a: dhammūpasañhitaṁpi cettha gītaṁ na vaṭṭati (songs concerning dhamma are not allowed)
            • what is dhammūpasañhitaṁ gītaṁ?
              • no examples are given, but it is generally accepted that it means songs made about dhamma or with dhamma words, played with instruments, drawn out voices, choirs, in a song-like manner, leading to the 5 disadvantages
        • allowances
          • anujānāmi, bhikkhave, sarabhaññaṁ
            • 32 sarabhañña: such as taraṅgavatta, taraṅgabhedavatta, and galitavatta - but they are not explained - srtdpntk: taraṃgavattādīnaṃ uccāraṇa(utterance)vidhānāni(performance) naṭṭha(lost)ppayogānī (at that time there were no performances of these vattas, no one knew it)
          • kdkpt-a: gītūpasañhito pana dhammo vaṭṭati (dhamma in a melodious way is allowed)
            • what is gītūpasañhito dhammo?
              • no examples are given, but it is generally accepted that it means dhamma recited in a melodious way, respecting the mattās, without instruments and choirs, without distorting words and meanings, and that does not lead to the 5 disadvantages
              • note: in sīlakkhandhavaggaṭīka the opposite is given

        • ‒Designs‒
          • After gathering information about the parissāvanas, the group brainstormed different designs for each parissāvana, and settled for one design for dhamakaraṇa and two for kaṭacchuparissāvana.
          • Dhamakaraṇa: an umbrella-shaped vessel with a small orifice on its top, and a filter attached to its rim.
          • Kaṭacchuparissāvana: considering the short description of it being made with three sticks, various designs could be made. The group decided on 2 models.
            • Model 1: Instead of a triangular strainer, made by binding three sticks, the group settled for a racket-like parissāvana with a stirdy handle and a creative design that uses precise partial cuts to allow bamboo to be bent, and makes the model free from joints, made of a single piece of bamboo.
            • Model 2: A more sophisticated three-layered filtration system on a mobile tripod that does not require digging, can be carried around fairly easily, and possesses multiple other practical applications in the forest. It comes with a convenient bag with a shoulder strap for the canes and another for the filtering material.
        • ‒Gathering materials‒
          • The materials were selected for each model considering sustainability, environment-friendliness, availability, and durability.
          • The Dhamakaraṇa was chosen to be made of the hard shell (endocarp) of coconut, taking advantage of its natural curved shape and availability.
          • The Kaṭacchuparissāvana Model 1 was chosen to be made of a single piece of cured bamboo, using a creative way of partially cutting it, allowing it to be bent in a circular shape, while retaining its strength.
          • The Kaṭacchuparissāvana Model 2 was chosen to be made of three bamboo canes cured with fire to remove natural oils that make it less durable and subject to termite and bug attacks. The natural indentations in each joint of the canes facilitates the tying of the filters bearing the water-purifying materials. The wood charcoal was directly collected from the logs to avoid contamination. The sand was collected from the natural stream and desinfected.
          • All the joints were handmade using a simple yet efficacious technique for turning reeds or grass into strong natural ropes by partially drying them and twisting them together.
          • The filters were made of two layers of natural undyed cloth according to availability.

        • ‒Instructions‒
          • How to use the Dhamakaraṇa
            • The dhamakaraṇa is a simple yet effective tool for filtrating small amounts of water for personal use making use of the force of vaccum. Place the cloth filter firmly covering its mouth, and while keeping its mouth downwards, submerge it partially in water. The water goes through the filter filling the hollow body. With a finger, close the orifice on top of the filter, and then raise the dhamakaraṇa from the water. Due to the vaccum created, the water remains in the body of the dhamakaraṇa and can be then released into a clean container either by uncovering the orifice or by turning it upside down and allowing the filtrated water to ooze out from the orifice. The filter can be easily removed and washed regularly.
          • How to use the Kaṭacchuparissāvana Model 1
            • The first model of kaṭacchuparissāvana requires little explanation and can be used by simply pouring water through it into a clean container. It can filter fairly large amounts of water, and is easy to wash and carry around. It comes with a convenient handmande rope attached to its handle.
          • How to use the Kaṭacchuparissāvana Model 2
            • The third model can be carried around in a bag with a shoulder strap. It can be prepared easily by spreading out the three canes evenly into a tripod, fastening the three filters in place, and pouring water from the top. The water goes through the filters, which act as pockets that can be filled with purifying material for the water. In this case, the three pockets are filled with sand, wood charcoal and cotton wool. A clean vessel should be placed under the filters to collect the filtrated water. The filters can be easily untied and washed, and the filtering material removed or replaced. Without the filters, the tripod can double as a stand for hanging pots, boiling water, holding poles, protecting fires, breaking winds, etc.
